Math Enthusiast is organized around **questions, discoveries, and mathematical ideas** rather than a conventional sequence of technical chapters. Use this outline as a map of the collection.

# Learning Outcomes

For the **undergraduate seminar** version of this course (see `course_passport.yaml`), students who complete the work should be able to:

1. **Explain** a major open or recently resolved mathematical problem: precise statement, why it is hard, and one theory grown around it (without solving it).
2. **Distinguish** notions of mathematical size (e.g. measure vs dimension) and avoid common confusions such as “measure zero ⇒ lower dimension.”
3. **Reconstruct** the *idea* of a famous proof or breakthrough in a short narrative a peer can follow.
4. **Relate** a classical field on the course map to a named modern application, with a correct one-step mechanism.
5. **Design and document** a small mathematical exploration (question, experiments, including failed attempts).
6. **Critique** popular writing about mathematics for precision versus hype.

Self-learners using the public site can treat these as optional goals; graded deliverables apply when the seminar is taught. Seminar students: see repository files `syllabus.md` and `assignments/` for the weekly plan, weights, and briefs.

## Course Outline

### 1. Great Problems in Mathematics

Explore famous mathematical problems that challenged generations of mathematicians.

- The Riemann Hypothesis
- The P vs NP Problem
- The Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer Conjecture
- The Navier–Stokes problem
- The Twin Prime Conjecture
- The Collatz Conjecture
- The Kakeya Conjecture
- The Four Color Theorem

The goal is not necessarily to solve these problems, but to understand **what the problem is, why it is difficult, and what mathematics has been developed around it.**

---

### 2. Fields Medal & Modern Mathematics

Discover the mathematics behind the **Fields Medal**—not the award itself, but the ideas that led to Fields Medal–winning work.

Themes include number theory, algebraic geometry, topology, differential geometry, PDEs, combinatorics, probability, mathematical physics, representation theory, dynamical systems, analytic number theory, and geometric analysis.

Examples:

- The Langlands Program
- Perelman’s work on the Poincaré Conjecture
- Green–Tao theorem on arithmetic progressions
- Mirzakhani’s work on geometry and moduli spaces
- Scholze’s perfectoid spaces
- Venkatesh’s work connecting number theory and representation theory
- Viazovska’s sphere-packing breakthrough
- Maynard’s work on prime numbers
- Birkar’s work in algebraic geometry
- Recent developments in combinatorics and geometry
- 2026 Fields portraits (e.g. Wang, Deng)

---

### 8. Abel Prize & Lifetime Mathematics

Discover the mathematics behind the **Abel Prize**—lifetime-scale contributions without an age limit (a “Nobel-like” honor for mathematics).

Examples:

- Atiyah & Singer — the index theorem bridging analysis and topology (2004)
- Andrew Wiles — modularity and Fermat’s Last Theorem (2016)
- Karen Uhlenbeck — geometric analysis and gauge theory (2019)
- Furstenberg & Margulis — dynamics and rigidity (2020)
- Lovász & Wigderson — discrete math and theoretical CS (2021)
- Dennis Sullivan — topology and dynamics (2022)
- Luis Caffarelli — free boundaries and nonlinear PDE (2023)
- Michel Talagrand — concentration of measure and probability (2024)
- Masaki Kashiwara — $$D$$-modules and crystal bases (2025)

Compare with Chapter 2 (Fields): breakthroughs under 40 vs career-scale bodies of work.

---

### 9. Turing Award & Computational Mathematics

Discover the mathematics behind the **A.M. Turing Award**—ACM’s highest honor in computing, often called the “Nobel Prize of computing”—not as a list of laureates, but as **ideas that rewired algorithms, complexity, cryptography, learning, and proof**.

Themes include computability, NP-completeness, analysis of algorithms, public-key cryptography, zero-knowledge proofs, communication complexity, learning theory, graph algorithms, causal inference, deep learning foundations, and randomness in computation.

Examples:

- What the Turing Award is (and how it differs from Fields / Abel)
- Turing, computability, and undecidability
- Cook, Karp, and NP-completeness
- Knuth and the analysis of algorithms
- Public-key cryptography (Diffie–Hellman, RSA)
- Goldwasser, Micali, and cryptographic foundations
- Yao’s communication complexity and minimax principle
- Valiant and computational learning theory
- Hopcroft, Tarjan, and algorithmic graph theory
- Pearl and causal reasoning
- Bengio, Hinton, LeCun and deep learning
- Wigderson and the role of randomness in complexity (Turing 2023; Abel 2021 with Lovász)

Compare with Chapter 2 (Fields) and Chapter 8 (Abel): three different spotlights on living mathematical science—geometry/analysis/number theory breakthroughs, lifetime pure-math arcs, and the computational mathematics that powers modern technology.

### 3. Mathematics That Changed the World

Mathematical ideas that became foundations for modern technology and science.

- Calculus → Physics & Engineering
- Linear Algebra → AI & Machine Learning
- Probability → Data Science
- Number Theory → Cryptography
- Graph Theory → Networks & Algorithms
- Optimization → Operations & AI
- Fourier Analysis → Signal Processing
- Differential Equations → Physics & Engineering

---

### 4. Beautiful Mathematics

Mathematics that is fascinating simply because of the ideas themselves.

- Infinity, fractals, symmetry, chaos
- Strange geometric objects, paradoxes, higher dimensions
- Minimal surfaces, tilings, impossible shapes, emergence

---

### 5. Mathematics Through Famous Proofs

Learn by following remarkable proofs and discoveries—focusing on the **idea behind the proof**.

- Euclid’s proof of infinitely many primes
- Cantor’s diagonal argument
- Gödel’s incompleteness theorems
- Euler’s solution to the Königsberg bridges
- The irrationality of $$\sqrt{2}$$
- The Four Color Theorem
- Fermat’s Last Theorem
- The Poincaré Conjecture

---

### 6. Mathematics of the Future

Areas where mathematics is developing rapidly.

- Mathematics of AI and machine learning theory
- Quantum information
- Mathematical biology
- Network science and cryptography
- Complexity theory and high-dimensional geometry
- Optimal transport and mathematical physics

---

### 7. Mathematical Explorations

Open-ended investigations where there may be no immediate answer.

- Smallest Kakeya sets and efficient sphere packing
- Predictability of primes and order from randomness
- Visualizing a fourth dimension
- Map coloring, infinity, and iterated dynamical rules

This section encourages you to **experiment, conjecture, visualize, and reason**.

## How the Pieces Fit Together

| Path | What you gain |
|------|----------------|
| Famous Problems | A sense of what remains open and why it matters |
| Fields & breakthroughs | How research fields grow from hard questions |
| Abel lifetime arcs | How decades of tools reshape a climate of thought |
| Turing / computational math | How precise ideas about algorithms and information become technology |
| World-changing math | Why abstraction powers technology |
| Beautiful ideas | Pure curiosity without an application test |
| Famous proofs | The craft of mathematical argument |
| Future frontiers | Where new mathematics is being built now |
| Explorations | Practice thinking like a mathematician |
