# Welcome to Math Enthusiast

**Explore the ideas, problems, and discoveries that make modern mathematics fascinating.**

Math Enthusiast is a collection of mathematical topics for learners who want to go beyond school mathematics and discover how mathematicians think, what major problems they work on, and how seemingly abstract ideas connect to the real world.

This is not a traditional textbook ordered by technical prerequisites. It is a map of **modern mathematics** organized around questions, discoveries, and ideas—so you can find the regions of mathematics that pull you in and decide what to study more deeply.

## The Core Idea

Math Enthusiast follows a path from famous open problems to living fields of research:

**Famous Problems → Mathematical Fields → Breakthroughs → Beautiful Ideas → Open Questions**

Along the way you will meet theorems that reshaped technology, proofs that rewired how we reason about infinity and truth, and questions that remain unsolved after centuries of effort.

## Seminar vs self-study

This site is both a **public map** of modern mathematics and the reading base for an **undergraduate seminar with deliverables**. When taught as a seminar:

- Weekly meetings center on **one flagship deep read per section** (not every short page).
- Graded work includes reading responses, an open-problem brief, a proof-idea narrative, an exploration portfolio, and a synthesis essay (see `course_passport.yaml`).
- Bilingual materials (English / Vietnamese) support language switching; instruction language is set by the offering.

## What You Will Find Here

- **Great problems** — Riemann, P vs NP, Navier–Stokes, twin primes, Collatz, and more: what they ask, why they resist solution, and what mathematics grew around them.
- **Fields Medal mathematics** — the ideas behind landmark work in number theory, geometry, topology, analysis, and combinatorics.
- **Abel Prize mathematics** — lifetime contributions that reshaped probability, PDE, topology, dynamics, discrete math, and representation theory (no age limit; “Nobel-like” scale).
- **Turing Award mathematics** — computational ideas honored by ACM’s highest award: complexity, algorithms, cryptography, learning, causality, and the theory of computation.
- **Mathematics that changed the world** — calculus, linear algebra, probability, cryptography, networks, optimization, Fourier analysis, and differential equations as foundations of science and technology.
- **Beautiful mathematics** — infinity, fractals, symmetry, chaos, higher dimensions, paradoxes, and other ideas that are rewarding for their own sake.
- **Famous proofs** — Euclid, Cantor, Gödel, Euler, Fermat’s Last Theorem, the Poincaré Conjecture, and more, with emphasis on the *idea* of the proof.
- **Mathematics of the future** — AI, quantum information, networks, optimal transport, complexity, and mathematical physics.
- **Open explorations** — investigations where you experiment, conjecture, and reason, rather than only complete predefined exercises.

## How to Use This Course

**If you are in the taught seminar:** use the course repository files `syllabus.md` and `assignments/` (flagship deep reads + deliverables). Instructors: design of record in `course_passport.yaml`. Browse short map pages for context; grade-bearing work targets the learning outcomes, not every page.

**If you are self-studying:**

1. Start from the [course outline]({{ site.baseurl }}/) and browse the sections in the sidebar (including Fields, Abel, and Turing prize maps).
2. Jump by curiosity—not only by chapter order. Language-switch links keep matching topics aligned across English and Vietnamese when both exist.
3. For each hard problem or proof, aim to understand **what is being asked**, **why it is difficult**, and **what ideas it generates**, not merely a formal statement.
4. Use the Explorations section to try small experiments, draw pictures, form conjectures, and test them.

## Prerequisites

**Seminar baseline:** about one year of university calculus (or equivalent) and willingness to engage with proofs at an introductory level. Research-level background is not required.

Many shorter essays are still accessible with high-school algebra and careful reading. Deeper flagships introduce vocabulary as needed.

## A Note on Goals

The objective is not to “finish” modern mathematics—that is impossible—but to give you a working map of the landscape and the taste of how research mathematics feels: open questions, partial progress, sudden breakthroughs, and ideas that connect far-apart fields.

Let’s begin.
