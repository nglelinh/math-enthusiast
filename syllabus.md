## What this course is about

Math Enthusiast is a map of **modern mathematics** organized around questions and discoveries: famous open problems, Fields Medal–level ideas, mathematics that powers technology, beautiful pure ideas, famous proof ideas, research frontiers, and open-ended explorations.

This offering is an **undergraduate seminar with deliverables**, not a computational methods course and not a survey that requires every site page. Each week centers on **one flagship deep read**, seminar discussion, and writing that trains six learning outcomes: explain hard problems, distinguish notions of size, reconstruct proof ideas, connect fields to applications, explore experimentally, and critique popular math writing.

---

## Learning outcomes

By the end of this course, you will be able to:

1. **(LO1)** Explain a major open (or recently resolved) mathematical problem: state it precisely enough for a peer, identify why it is difficult, and name at least one mathematical tool or theory developed around it—without claiming a full solution.
2. **(LO2)** Distinguish and compare different notions of mathematical “size” (e.g. Lebesgue measure vs Hausdorff/Minkowski dimension) using Kakeya sets or another course example, and correct the misconception that measure zero implies lower dimension.
3. **(LO3)** Reconstruct the core idea of a famous proof or breakthrough in a short narrative a classmate could follow, distinguishing idea from technical detail.
4. **(LO4)** Relate a classical mathematical field from the course map to a named modern application or technology, with a correct one-step mechanism (not only a buzzword).
5. **(LO5)** Design and carry out a small mathematical exploration: formulate a question or conjecture, document experiments or examples, and report what was learned including failed attempts.
6. **(LO6)** Critique a popular or secondary exposition of a mathematical breakthrough for precision versus hype, citing at least one place where wording overstates or understates the theorem.

## How this course works

**In seminar (~2.5 h/week).** Discussion of the flagship reading, board work on definitions and ideas, peer feedback, studio time later in the term. One discussion leadership turn per student (A1).

**Outside seminar (~5 h/week typical).** Deep read the flagship; short map pages only when assigned; write reading responses and major papers; run exploration experiments in weeks 11–15. Total effort aims near **7–8 hours/week** including class.

**Flagship-per-section rule.** You are not graded on covering every short page. You are graded on outcomes evidenced in discussion and writing.
