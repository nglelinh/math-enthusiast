A theorem without a proof is a rumor. A proof without an idea is a tax return.

This chapter is about **ideas that became arguments**—the short, bright skeletons that turn a claim into knowledge. Euclid shows that any finite list of primes is incomplete. Cantor shows that any list of reals is incomplete. Gödel shows that any rich formal system is incomplete in another sense. Euler turns seven bridges into the birth of graph theory. A diagonal or a clever product-plus-one can change a subject forever.

We will not worship length. Some of the most important proofs in history fit on a page once the idea is clear. Others—Fermat’s Last Theorem, the Poincaré conjecture—are modern epics, but even they have a **core idea** you can carry home: a bridge between fields, a flow that smooths geometry, a modularity that forbids a Diophantine accident.

Read for the *move*: the construction, the contradiction, the escape from an enumeration, the model that makes the impossible visible.

When you can retell a proof’s idea to a classmate without looking at symbols, you have done more than study history. You have practiced the craft that every mathematician, in every century, actually lives by.
