In the early 1970s, **Stephen Cook** and **Richard Karp** (with independent related work by Leonid Levin) transformed the vague complaint “this combinatorial search feels hard” into a **mathematical classification**. The **Cook–Levin theorem** showed that Boolean satisfiability (**SAT**) is **NP-complete**: it is a hardest problem in NP under efficient reductions. Karp then exhibited a famous list of natural problems—clique, vertex cover, Hamiltonian cycle, partition, and more—each polynomial-time reducible from SAT or from each other, revealing an interconnected **zoo** of complete problems.

Cook received the **ACM A.M. Turing Award in 1982** for foundational contributions to complexity theory, notably the NP-completeness of SAT. Karp received the **Turing Award in 1985** for contributions to the theory of algorithms, including the development of NP-completeness methodology and the identification of many complete problems. Official materials: [amturing.acm.org](https://amturing.acm.org/).

This lecture focuses on **mechanisms**: certificates, reductions, completeness, and why P vs NP remains open even after a mature theory—and why **practical SAT solvers** can still be miracles of engineering without settling the Millennium question.

## 1. From “feels hard” to NP

A **decision problem** asks a yes/no question about a finite input. **P** collects problems solvable in deterministic time polynomial in the input length. **NP** collects problems where every **yes** instance has a short **certificate** (witness) checkable in polynomial time, and no false certificate fools the checker on **no** instances.

Examples in NP: SAT (assignment as certificate), Hamiltonian cycle (the cycle itself), 3-colorability (the coloring), subset sum (the subset). Clearly $$\mathbf{P} \subseteq \mathbf{NP}$$: if you can solve the problem outright, verification is easy. The open question is whether the inclusion reverses.

Before NP-completeness, hardness was anecdotal. After NP-completeness, hardness became **transferable**.

---

## 2. Polynomial-time reductions

A language $$A$$ is **polynomial-time (Karp) reducible** to a language $$B$$, written

$$
A \le_p B,
$$

if there is a poly-time computable function $$f$$ such that for every input $$x$$,

$$
x \in A \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad f(x) \in B.
$$

Intuition: $$B$$ is at least as hard as $$A$$, up to polynomial overhead. If $$B$$ were in P, then $$A$$ would be in P: compute $$f(x)$$ and decide membership in $$B$$.

**Karp reductions** (many-one reductions) map instances to instances. Cook’s original framework also used **Turing reductions** (adaptive queries to an oracle for $$B$$). For NP-completeness culture in undergraduate mathematics, Karp reductions are the standard glue.

Reductions are the **morphisms** of complexity theory. Designing a reduction is a creative mathematical act: encode one combinatorial structure inside another without blowing up size superpolynomially.

---

## 3. NP-hardness and NP-completeness

A language $$L$$ is **NP-hard** if every language in NP reduces to $$L$$ in polynomial time. It is **NP-complete** if it is in NP and NP-hard.

Completeness is a **upper and lower** sandwich: $$L$$ is not harder than NP (it sits inside), yet it is hard enough that an efficient algorithm for $$L$$ would efficiently solve *all* of NP.

**Corollary.** If any NP-complete problem lies in P, then $$\mathbf{P} = \mathbf{NP}$$. If $$\mathbf{P} \ne \mathbf{NP}$$, then no NP-complete problem lies in P. The zoo stands or falls together.

---

## 4. Cook–Levin: SAT is NP-complete

**Boolean satisfiability (SAT):** given a propositional formula $$\varphi$$ (typically in conjunctive normal form), does there exist a truth assignment making $$\varphi$$ true?

SAT is clearly in NP: guess an assignment, evaluate $$\varphi$$ in poly time.

**Cook–Levin theorem:** SAT is NP-hard, hence NP-complete.

**Proof idea (cartoon).** Take any language $$A \in \mathbf{NP}$$. There is a poly-time verifier $$V(x,w)$$ that accepts some short witness $$w$$ iff $$x \in A$$. The computation of $$V$$ on $$(x,w)$$ is a poly-size tableau of configurations (or a circuit/unrolled circuit). Encode “there exists a witness string and a valid accepting computation tableau” as a Boolean formula $$\varphi_x$$ whose satisfiability is equivalent to $$x \in A$$. The encoding is uniform and poly-time in $$|x|$$. Thus $$A \le_p \textsc{SAT}$$.

Once one complete problem exists, **others follow by reduction chains**, not by replaying the full tableau argument each time.

## 5. Karp’s zoo and the 21 problems

In 1972, Karp published a landmark paper exhibiting **21 NP-complete problems**, including:

- **CLIQUE** / **INDEPENDENT SET** / **VERTEX COVER** (graph packing and covering);  
- **CHROMATIC NUMBER** decisions (graph coloring);  
- **HAMILTONIAN CYCLE** (and related TSP decision forms);  
- **PARTITION**, **KNAPSACK** decision versions;  
- **EXACT COVER**, **3-DIMENSIONAL MATCHING**;  
- and other staples of combinatorial optimization.

The method is always: assume a known complete problem (often 3-SAT), design a gadget-filled construction that translates yes-instances to yes-instances, and verify poly-time constructibility. Garey and Johnson’s later encyclopedia turned this into a reference culture: thousands of problems classified by reductions.

**Why this mattered scientifically.** Operations research, graph theory, and scheduling suddenly shared a **common hardness currency**. A new problem could inherit decades of failed poly-time attempts by linking into the web—without solving P vs NP.

## 6. Why P vs NP remains open

NP-completeness organizes the world; it does not settle whether $$\mathbf{P} = \mathbf{NP}$$. Barriers explain why many natural proof strategies fail (relativization, natural proofs, algebrization—at slogan level in Chapter 01). Most experts conjecture $$\mathbf{P} \ne \mathbf{NP}$$, which would mean every NP-complete problem lacks a poly-time algorithm. The conjecture is not a theorem.

Important subtleties for mathematicians:

- **Average-case** vs **worst-case**: crypto often needs hardness on random instances; classical NP-completeness is worst-case.  
- **Approximation**: even if exact optimization is hard, approximate solutions may be poly-time—or hard to approximate (a refined theory).  
- **Parameterized algorithms**, **fine-grained complexity**, and **circuit lower bounds** are active research frontiers beyond the 1970s core.

Abel 2021 (Lovász–Wigderson) celebrates discrete math and TCS as central pure mathematics **without** claiming P vs NP is solved—same intellectual honesty applies to Cook and Karp’s Turing Awards.

---

## 7. Practical solvers (LO6)

Industrial **SAT solvers**, ILP solvers, and constraint solvers routinely crush instances with millions of variables that look “exponential” on paper. How is that compatible with NP-completeness?

- Completeness is about **worst-case asymptotic** existence of hard families, not about every instance you meet in a fab plant or verification suite.  
- Real instances have **structure** (sparsity, symmetry, community structure) that heuristics and CDCL SAT solvers exploit.  
- Timeouts, incomplete search, and approximation are part of practice; theory’s yes/no worst-case poly-time question is stricter.  
- Breakthrough solver engineering is genuine progress—and orthogonal to a proof that $$\mathbf{P} = \mathbf{NP}$$.

**LO6 habit:** when a headline says “AI solves NP-complete problem,” ask: *which family of instances? worst-case guarantees? decision vs search? comparison to existing SAT solvers?* Membership in a complexity class is not a vibe about workplace difficulty.

---

## 8. Two Turing Awards, one mathematical revolution

**Cook (1982)** crystallized the completeness of SAT and helped define the P vs NP question as a central scientific problem. **Karp (1985)** industrialized the method: reductions as a research practice, algorithms for combinatorial optimization, and a map of hardness across natural problems. Levin’s independent work on universal search and completeness is part of the same historical knot (often acknowledged as Cook–Levin).

Together they exemplify Turing citation culture at its most mathematical: not a product launch, but a **definition of hardness** that still structures textbooks half a century later.

## 9. Confusions

| Claim | Correction |
|-------|------------|
| “NP means not polynomial.” | NP means poly-time **verifiable** (with a witness), not “non-polynomial.” |
| “NP-complete means undecidable.” | NP-complete problems are decidable (brute force); the issue is efficient algorithms. |
| “Cook proved P ≠ NP.” | Cook proved SAT is NP-complete; P vs NP is open. |
| “Karp reductions are the only reductions.” | They are the standard many-one notion for NP-completeness; other reductions exist. |
| “A fast SAT solver on my benchmark proves P = NP.” | Benchmarks are not universal quantifiers over all instances and all sizes. |
