Once a year, the Association for Computing Machinery (ACM) awards the **A.M. Turing Award**—often called the **Nobel Prize of computing**. The medal is named after Alan Mathison Turing, whose 1936 analysis of “computable numbers” and wartime work on machines and codes helped invent the conceptual world we now call computer science.

This chapter is **not** a banquet of résumé lines. It is a guided walk through **ideas that rewired how we think about computation, algorithms, cryptography, learning, and proof**—ideas that mathematicians and computer scientists now share as common air.

You will meet stories with a shape familiar from the Fields and Abel chapters: a stubborn question; a vocabulary that made the question precise; a breakthrough that let other people do new work; and a human career that carried the idea into daylight. The difference is the *stage*. Here the objects are Turing machines and circuits, one-way functions and NP-complete problems, PAC learnability and causal graphs, communication complexity and random bits, neural networks and public keys.

Some essays sit next to Chapter 1’s [P vs NP]({{ site.baseurl }}/contents/en/chapter01/01_03_P_vs_NP/) and Chapter 6’s complexity and AI map. Others sit next to Chapter 3’s [cryptography]({{ site.baseurl }}/contents/en/chapter03/03_05_Number_Theory_Cryptography/) and Chapter 8’s [Wigderson]({{ site.baseurl }}/contents/en/chapter08/08_06_Lovasz_Wigderson/) Abel portrait. Read for **mechanisms**, not for award-year trivia.

The prize is a spotlight. The mathematics of computation is the stage.
