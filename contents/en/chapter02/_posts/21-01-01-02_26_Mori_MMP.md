**Shigefumi Mori** received the **Fields Medal 1990** at ICM Kyoto. The year has no modern one-line IMU citation of the 1998–2006 type; the standard account is **Heisuke Hironaka**’s ICM lecture “On the work of Shigefumi Mori.” Hironaka describes the **minimal model program**—also called **Mori’s program**—for the birational classification of **algebraic threefolds**, and he records the earlier proof of **Hartshorne’s conjecture**: the only smooth complete algebraic varieties with ample tangent bundle are projective spaces. This course treats those two achievements as one architectural story: positivity of the tangent sheaf characterizes $$\mathbb{P}^n$$, and positivity (or negativity) of the canonical class organizes a reduction process in dimension three.

This essay is for learners who have seen curves and surfaces in a first algebraic geometry course and want the **foundation** that later higher-dimensional MMP extends. It does **not** say that Mori finished the minimal model program in all dimensions. That later story includes Kawamata, Kollár, Reid, Shokurov, the **Birkar–Cascini–Hacon–McKernan** theorems, and [Birkar’s Fano boundedness]({{ site.baseurl }}/contents/en/chapter02/02_10_Birkar_Algebraic_Geometry/). Mori’s 3-fold flips (1988) are the existence theorem that made dimension three a completed program in Hironaka’s sense.

## Learning objectives

After this lecture you should be able to:

- State **Hartshorne’s conjecture / Mori’s theorem**: a smooth projective variety with **ample tangent bundle** is isomorphic to projective space.
- Explain **bend-and-break** at slogan level: deforming a curve while fixing a point produces a rational curve.
- Describe the **3-dimensional MMP**: extremal rays, the **cone theorem**, contractions, and the **existence of flips** in dimension 3 (Mori, *J. Amer. Math. Soc.* 1988).
- Distinguish a **minimal model** ($$K$$ nef) from a **Mori fiber space** (Fano-like fibers).
- Attribute credit carefully: Kawamata, Kollár, Reid, Shokurov, and others built cone theorems, singularities, and termination; Mori proved 3-fold flips.
- Cross-link [Birkar]({{ site.baseurl }}/contents/en/chapter02/02_10_Birkar_Algebraic_Geometry/) as the later higher-dimensional chapter, not as a replacement for 1990.

**Prerequisites.** Projective varieties over $$\mathbb{C}$$ (or an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero) at first-course level; line bundles and ampleness; the canonical class $$K_X$$ as “algebraic curvature.” The [Birkar lecture]({{ site.baseurl }}/contents/en/chapter02/02_10_Birkar_Algebraic_Geometry/) may be read before or after: it assumes this foundation.

**Seminar links.** LO1 / LO4 (classical classification ↔ modern reduction). Pair with Birkar for the higher-dimensional sequel, and with Enriques–Kodaira surface classification as the dimension-two ancestor.

## 1. From surfaces to threefolds

Compact Riemann surfaces are organized by genus. Algebraic surfaces, after Castelnuovo, Enriques, Kodaira, and Zariski, are organized by Kodaira dimension and a finite list of geometric types, once one allows birational modifications (blow-ups and contractions of $$(-1)$$-curves). In dimension three the same hope—“list types after birational reduction”—meets new phenomena. Contractions that improve $$K_X$$ may fail to be divisorial: the locus one wants to replace can have codimension two, and no morphism simply blows it down. One needs **flips**.

Hironaka’s ICM text calls the resulting program the most profound development of the decade in algebraic geometry: a *constructive* reduction of a threefold, by elementary birational steps, toward a model whose canonical class is nef, or toward a fibration with Fano-like fibers. “Constructive” here means that the steps are in principle describable (divisorial contractions, flips, flops), not that a computer enumerates isomorphism types.

## 2. Hartshorne’s conjecture and Mori’s theorem

**Hartshorne** (around 1970) conjectured that a smooth projective (or smooth complete) variety whose **tangent bundle** $$T_X$$ is **ample** must be isomorphic to projective space $$\mathbb{P}^n$$. Ampleness of $$T_X$$ is a strong positivity: every quotient of $$T_X$$ is ample, and in a differential-geometric cousin (Frankel’s conjecture) positive holomorphic bisectional curvature forces a manifold to be $$\mathbb{P}^n$$. **Siu–Yau** (1980) proved the complex-geometric Frankel conjecture by harmonic maps; Mori proved the algebraic Hartshorne conjecture by a different route.

**Mori’s theorem** (*Ann. of Math.* **110**, 1979; the work dates to the late 1970s): if $$X$$ is a smooth projective variety and $$T_X$$ is ample, then $$X\simeq\mathbb{P}^n$$.

The method introduced **bend-and-break**. If a curve of positive genus deforms nontrivially while passing through a fixed point, the deformation “breaks” and produces a rational curve through that point. On a Fano manifold ($$-K_X$$ ample) one obtains rational curves of bounded $$K$$-degree through every point: Fano manifolds are **uniruled**. Ample tangent bundle is stronger than ample $$-K_X$$ (since $$-K_X=\det T_X$$), and the geometry of **minimal rational tangents** forces the variety to be projective space.

**Slogan.** Extreme positivity of the tangent sheaf leaves no room for any shape except $$\mathbb{P}^n$$.

This is a characterization theorem, not a classification of all Fano varieties. Fano threefolds with Picard number at least two were later classified by **Mori–Mukai** (1981, with a later erratum); that is a neighboring census, not the Hartshorne statement.

## 3. Extremal rays and the cone theorem

Let $$X$$ be a smooth projective variety (later: a mild singular threefold). Curves on $$X$$ generate a cone $$\overline{\mathrm{NE}}(X)$$ in the real vector space of numerical 1-cycles. The canonical class $$K_X$$ is a linear function on that space. The **$$K$$-negative part** of the cone is locally polyhedral: it is spanned by finitely many **extremal rays** away from the hyperplane $$K_X=0$$. Each extremal ray is generated by a rational curve. This is the **cone theorem**, developed by Mori in the early 1980s (the 1982 *Annals* paper on threefolds whose canonical bundles are not nef is a landmark) and extended in the log setting by **Kawamata, Kollár, Reid, Shokurov**, and others.

Given an extremal ray $$R$$, the **contraction theorem** produces a morphism $$\varphi:X\to Y$$ that contracts precisely the curves in $$R$$. Three pictures appear:

- **Fiber-type contraction.** $$\dim Y<\dim X$$: the general fiber is Fano-like. One has reached a **Mori fiber space**.
- **Divisorial contraction.** A divisor is contracted; Picard number drops by one; one continues the program on $$Y$$.
- **Small contraction.** The exceptional locus has codimension at least two. There is no divisorial blow-down. One needs a **flip**.

**Slogan.** The cone theorem finds the negative directions of $$K$$; the contraction theorem tries to spend them. When spending would require surgery in codimension two, the flip is the surgery.

## 4. Flips in dimension three

A **flip** replaces a small contraction $$\varphi:X\to Y$$ (with $$-K_X$$ relatively ample) by another small birational morphism $$\varphi^+:X^+\to Y$$ such that $$K_{X^+}$$ is relatively ample. The varieties $$X$$ and $$X^+$$ are isomorphic in codimension one; the canonical class changes sign relative to $$Y$$. Existence of $$X^+$$ is not formal. In dimension three, Mori proved it.

**Mori’s flip theorem** (*J. Amer. Math. Soc.* **1**, 1988, “Flip theorem and the existence of minimal models for 3-folds”): for a small contraction of a projective 3-fold with $$\mathbb{Q}$$-factorial terminal singularities, the flip exists, and the flipped variety again has $$\mathbb{Q}$$-factorial terminal singularities. Combined with **Shokurov**’s **difficulty** invariant (which drops under flips, so they cannot continue forever) and the cone/contraction package, this yields the existence of minimal models (or Mori fiber spaces) for terminal $$\mathbb{Q}$$-factorial threefolds.

Hironaka’s lecture stresses that Mori “finished up the program by himself overcoming the last difficulty”—the last difficulty being 3-fold flips—while crediting collaborators along the way. This course keeps both halves: Mori’s solo flip paper is the capstone of the *threefold* program; the cone, singularities, and termination are a community.

**Do not say:** Mori finished MMP in all dimensions. Existence of flips in higher dimensions was open for decades and became a theorem in broad settings only much later (notably **BCHM**). Termination of flips in high dimension remains subtle. [Birkar’s lecture]({{ site.baseurl }}/contents/en/chapter02/02_10_Birkar_Algebraic_Geometry/) is the place to see Fano boundedness and the higher-dimensional architecture.

## 5. What the program classifies—and what it does not

After running the 3-fold MMP one expects:

- a **minimal model**, with $$K$$ nef, when the variety is not uniruled in the relevant sense; or
- a **Mori fiber space**, when negativity of $$K$$ persists and produces a fibration.

That is classification *up to birational equivalence*, organized by the sign of $$K$$, not a list of isomorphism types. Terminal singularities are part of the language: the process cannot stay in the smooth category. Reid’s philosophy of canonical and terminal singularities, Kawamata’s vanishing and rationality theorems, Kollár’s contributions to the cone and to moduli, and Shokurov’s log flips and difficulty are the surrounding machine.

**Examples at slogan level.** Projective space is the unique smooth variety with ample tangent bundle (Hartshorne–Mori). A cubic threefold is Fano and sits on the “positive” side. A smooth hypersurface of high degree in $$\mathbb{P}^4$$ is of general type and should run toward a minimal model with $$K$$ big.

## 6. Why a Fields Medal

1. **A characterization that opened a method.** Hartshorne’s conjecture is a single elegant statement; bend-and-break became a tool for all later uniruledness theorems.
2. **A completed 3-fold program.** Existence of flips in dimension three turned MMP from a conjectural flowchart into a theorem for terminal threefolds.
3. **Foundation for the next generation.** Higher-dimensional MMP, BCHM finite generation, and Birkar’s boundedness of Fanos are sequels. The 1990 medal is the foundation chapter, not the last chapter.

Mori later served as President of the International Mathematical Union—the first from East Asia—an institutional coda, not a mathematical claim.

## Common confusions

| Claim | Correction |
|-------|------------|
| “Mori finished MMP in all dimensions.” | He completed the 3-fold program (flips in dimension 3). Higher dimensions came later (BCHM and others). |
| “Ample tangent bundle means Fano.” | Fano is ampleness of $$-K_X=\det T_X$$, which is weaker; ample $$T_X$$ forces $$\mathbb{P}^n$$. |
| “A flip is a blow-up.” | A flip replaces a small locus; it is not a classical blow-up of a divisor. |
| “Minimal model means smooth minimal surface.” | In dimension $$\ge 3$$, minimal models are allowed terminal singularities; $$K$$ nef is the goal. |
| “Hartshorne’s conjecture is still open.” | Mori proved it (1979 *Annals*). |
| “Birkar’s medal replaces Mori’s.” | Birkar extends the higher-dimensional architecture; the 3-fold foundation remains Mori’s. |
| “The cone theorem is only Mori’s.” | Mori’s 3-fold cone is foundational; Kawamata–Kollár–Reid–Shokurov developed the general/log package. |
