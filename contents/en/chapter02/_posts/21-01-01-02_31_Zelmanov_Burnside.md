**Efim Zelmanov** received the **Fields Medal 1994**

> “For the solution of the restricted Burnside problem.”  
> — [IMU, Fields Medals 1994](https://www.mathunion.org/imu-awards/fields-medal/fields-medals-1994)

Walter Feit’s ICM laudation states the same sentence: Zelmanov received the medal for the solution of the restricted Burnside problem. That adjective **restricted** is the entire pedagogical point of this essay. William Burnside asked in 1902 whether a finitely generated group in which every element has finite order must itself be finite. In the strongest “bounded exponent” form—every element satisfies $$x^n=1$$ for a fixed $$n$$—the general answer is **no**: there exist infinite finitely generated groups of finite exponent (Golod–Shafarevich for unbounded torsion; Novikov–Adian, Ol’shanskii, Ivanov, and others for large exponents). Zelmanov did **not** reverse those theorems. He answered a different question, formulated in the 1930s and named by Wilhelm Magnus: among **finite** groups, is there a uniform bound on the order of a $$d$$-generated group of exponent $$n$$? Equivalently: are there only finitely many finite $$d$$-generated groups of exponent $$n$$, up to isomorphism? The answer is **yes**, for all $$d$$ and all $$n$$. A. I. Kostrikin had treated prime exponent; Zelmanov treated odd exponents (1990) and then $$2$$-groups (1991), hence all exponents.

This essay is for learners who have met group presentations and want to keep three Burnside problems from collapsing into one slogan.

## Learning objectives

After this lecture you should be able to:

- State Burnside’s 1902 question: must a finitely generated periodic group be finite?
- Distinguish the **general** problem (periodic, no uniform exponent), the **bounded Burnside problem** (fixed exponent $$n$$), and the **restricted Burnside problem** (finiteness of the *finite* $$d$$-generated groups of exponent $$n$$).
- Name counterexamples at slogan level: **Golod–Shafarevich** (infinite finitely generated $$p$$-groups, orders not uniformly bounded); **Novikov–Adian** (infinite free Burnside groups $$B(d,n)$$ for large odd $$n$$); later even-exponent work of **Ivanov** and **Lysënok**; **Ol’shanskii**’s Tarski monsters.
- State Zelmanov’s theorem: for every $$d,n$$ there are only finitely many finite $$d$$-generated groups of exponent $$n$$ (equivalently, the restricted Burnside group is finite).
- Credit **Kostrikin** for the prime-exponent case of the restricted problem.
- Describe the method at slogan level: pass from groups to associated **Lie rings / Lie algebras**, using identities and, in the background, **Jordan algebras**.
- Avoid the false sentences “Zelmanov solved Burnside’s problem” and “all Burnside groups are finite.”

**Prerequisites.** Groups given by generators and relations; the difference between “every element has finite order” and “there is a uniform $$n$$ with $$x^n=1$$”; the idea of a Lie algebra as a bilinear bracket with Jacobi and $$[x,x]=0$$. No prior combinatorial group theory is assumed.

**Seminar links.** LO1 / LO6 (a classical question that split into several theorems; medal hype that drops the word “restricted”). Pair with other “one named problem, several meanings” portraits in this chapter, and with the Abel-side culture of long structural programs in [Chapter 8]({{ site.baseurl }}/contents/en/chapter08/).

## 1. Three problems that share a name

A group $$G$$ is **periodic** (or torsion) if every element has finite order. Finite groups are periodic; the Prüfer $$p$$-group is periodic and infinite, but not finitely generated. Burnside asked:

> If $$G$$ is finitely generated and periodic, must $$G$$ be finite?

That is the **general Burnside problem**. A sharper version fixes an exponent: there exists $$n$$ such that $$g^n=1$$ for all $$g\in G$$. The **free Burnside group** $$B(d,n)$$ is the largest $$d$$-generated group of exponent $$n$$ (the quotient of the free group $$F_d$$ by the fully invariant subgroup generated by all $$n$$th powers). The **bounded Burnside problem** asks: for which $$d,n$$ is $$B(d,n)$$ finite?

Easy positive cases were known early. $$B(1,n)$$ is cyclic of order $$n$$. $$B(d,2)$$ is elementary abelian of rank $$d$$. Burnside, Sanov, and Marshall Hall established finiteness of $$B(d,3)$$, $$B(d,4)$$, and $$B(d,6)$$. For most other small exponents—famously $$B(2,5)$$—the question remains open. For large exponents the answer is negative, as we recall in the next section.

The **restricted Burnside problem** changes the universe of discourse. It does not ask whether $$B(d,n)$$ is finite. It asks whether the **finite** quotients of $$B(d,n)$$ have bounded order—equivalently, whether there is a unique maximal finite $$d$$-generated group of exponent $$n$$, often denoted $$\widehat{B}(d,n)$$ or $$B_0(d,n)$$, obtained by quotienting out the intersection of all finite-index (equivalently, in this periodic setting, all finite) residual kernels. If that maximal finite quotient exists and is finite, then every finite $$d$$-generated group of exponent $$n$$ is a quotient of a single finite group, so there are only finitely many isomorphism types.

Zelmanov proved that this maximal finite quotient **is finite** for all $$d$$ and all $$n$$.

## 2. The unrestricted problems: the answer is no

In 1964, Evgeny Golod and Igor Shafarevich constructed infinite finitely generated $$p$$-groups (via a theorem on presentations of algebras). Those groups are periodic, so they kill the general Burnside problem, but the orders of elements are not bounded by a single $$n$$ fixed in advance.

The bounded-exponent problem required different technology. In 1968, Pyotr Novikov and Sergei Adian proved that $$B(d,n)$$ is infinite for $$d\ge 2$$ and odd $$n$$ larger than $$4381$$ (Adian later reduced the odd bound to $$665$$). Alexander Ol’shanskii gave geometric constructions of infinite finitely generated groups of large odd prime exponent in which every proper subgroup is cyclic (Tarski monsters), a particularly striking form of “infinite but locally cyclic.” The even-exponent case was harder: S. V. Ivanov (1994) proved infiniteness for large even exponents divisible by a high power of $$2$$; I. G. Lysënok (1996) improved the even bound. The upshot for this course:

**Many free Burnside groups $$B(d,n)$$ are infinite.** Zelmanov’s theorem is compatible with that fact. An infinite group can still have only finitely many finite quotients of a given type; more precisely, the finite residual of $$B(d,n)$$ can be huge while the finite quotient $$B_0(d,n)$$ remains finite.

Do not say “Burnside groups are finite.” Do not say “Zelmanov showed they are finite.”

## 3. The restricted problem: Kostrikin then Zelmanov

Kostrikin announced in the late 1950s that the restricted Burnside problem has a positive answer for **prime** exponent $$p$$: there is a largest finite $$d$$-generated group of exponent $$p$$. The argument is a theorem about Lie algebras in characteristic $$p$$ (Engel-type identities implying nilpotence in the associated Lie ring). Difficulties in the original treatment were later repaired; Kostrikin’s book *Around Burnside* is the standard account of that case, and it already points toward Zelmanov’s later work.

Zelmanov’s papers are:

- “Solution of the restricted Burnside problem for groups of odd exponent,” *Izv. Akad. Nauk SSSR Ser. Mat.* 54 (1990); English *Math. USSR-Izv.* 36 (1991), 41–60.
- “Solution of the restricted Burnside problem for $$2$$-groups,” *Mat. Sb.* 182 (1991); English *Math. USSR-Sb.* 72 (1992), 543–565.

Together with reduction lemmas that reduce a general exponent to a prime-power exponent (via Sylow and related finite-group arguments), these yield the restricted theorem for **all** $$n$$. Feit’s 1994 lecture emphasizes that Zelmanov first simplified and extended the Lie-algebraic engine beyond Kostrikin’s prime case, then treated the far harder $$2$$-primary situation.

## 4. Methods at slogan level: groups, Lie rings, Jordan algebras

The restricted problem is “to a large extent a problem in Lie algebras,” in Feit’s phrase. The dictionary is classical in the Russian school of Kostrikin, Magnus, Sanov, and Zelmanov:

1. Start with a finite $$d$$-generated group $$G$$ of exponent $$n$$. One wants a bound on $$\lvert G\rvert$$ depending only on $$d$$ and $$n$$.
2. Pass to an associated **Lie ring** (or a graded Lie algebra over a finite field) built from the lower central series, or from a Jennings–Zassenhaus $$p$$-series when $$n$$ is a prime power. Exponent $$n$$ becomes an **identity** in this Lie ring: an Engel-type condition, a restriction on ad-operators, or a sandwich identity.
3. Prove that a Lie algebra over a field of finite characteristic that satisfies those identities and is generated by $$d$$ elements must be **nilpotent** of bounded class, or at least finite-dimensional of bounded dimension.
4. Translate the Lie-algebra bound back into a bound on the order of $$G$$.

The hard step is (3). Zelmanov’s earlier work on **Jordan algebras** (infinite-dimensional structure; identities such as Glennie’s) supplied tools for nonassociative identities in the adjoint action, and he showed that the Engel identity implies nilpotence even in infinite dimension. This course will not reconstruct those identities. Slogan: restricted Burnside is a **finiteness theorem for Lie algebras with identities**, transferred to groups by the associated-Lie-ring machine—hence a group-theory medal resting on a Jordan-algebraic career.

## 5. What the theorem does and does not classify

The theorem is a **uniform bound**. It does not list the finite groups of exponent $$n$$, and effective bounds are typically enormous. It also does not decide which $$B(d,n)$$ are finite: the restricted solution can hold while $$B(d,n)$$ itself is infinite.

A useful comparison table:

| Question | Answer |
|----------|--------|
| Are all finitely generated periodic groups finite? | **No** (Golod–Shafarevich). |
| Are all $$B(d,n)$$ finite? | **No** for many large $$n$$; **yes** for some small $$n$$; some small cases **open**. |
| Are there only finitely many *finite* $$d$$-generated groups of exponent $$n$$? | **Yes**, all $$d,n$$ (Kostrikin for $$n=p$$; Zelmanov in general). |

The Fields citation is the third row.

## 6. Why a Fields Medal

Burnside’s question is among the oldest in combinatorial group theory. By 1994 the unrestricted problems had already displayed some of the wildest groups ever constructed, so a casual listener might have thought “Burnside is over.” The restricted problem had remained the finiteness question one could still hope to answer **positively**, and it had resisted for prime powers beyond Kostrikin’s $$p$$. Zelmanov closed it by deepening the Lie-theoretic and Jordan-theoretic toolkit rather than by a new geometric small-cancellation construction. The medal recognizes a completed classification-style finiteness theorem in a subject famous for counterexamples.

The 1994 class also included Bourgain, Lions, and Yoccoz. Zelmanov’s portrait is the algebra face of that Zürich congress.

## Common confusions

| Claim | Correction |
|-------|------------|
| “Zelmanov solved the Burnside problem.” | He solved the **restricted** Burnside problem. The unrestricted/bounded problems have negative answers in general. |
| “All groups of finite exponent are finite.” | False: $$B(d,n)$$ is infinite for many $$n$$. |
| “Golod–Shafarevich already settled everything.” | They killed the *general* (not uniformly bounded) problem. Restricted Burnside is about finite groups of fixed exponent. |
| “Kostrikin did the whole restricted problem.” | Kostrikin: **prime** exponent. Zelmanov: odd exponents and $$2$$-groups, hence all $$n$$. |
| “Restricted means ‘easier, so groups are finite.’” | Restricted means “ask only about **finite** groups.” The free Burnside group may still be infinite. |
| “The proof is purely group-theoretic combinatorics.” | The engine is **Lie rings / Lie algebras**, with Jordan-algebraic background. |
