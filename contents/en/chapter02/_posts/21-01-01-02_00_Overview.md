Every four years, at the International Congress of Mathematicians, a small group of mathematicians under forty receives the **Fields Medal**. The medals are not the point. What matters is the *work* the medals try to name: ideas so strong that they rearrange how a field thinks.

This chapter is a guided walk through that rearrangement.

You will not memorize prize years. You will meet **stories with a shape**: a problem that seemed immovable; a key idea that changed the grammar of the subject; a breakthrough that made other people able to do new things; and a human career that happened to be young enough, and bold enough, to carry the idea into daylight.

Some stories are legends you may have half-heard—Perelman and the shape of three-dimensional space; Wiles-adjacent modularity culture; Mirzakhani’s geometry of surfaces. Others are still warm from recent congresses: perfectoid spaces, prime gaps, sphere packing in magic dimensions, Kakeya in three-space, kinetic limits from particles and waves. Still others are older medals that this chapter now treats as dedicated portraits: Weil’s conjectures, viscosity solutions, restricted Burnside, exotic smooth four-space.

Read slowly. The prize is a spotlight. The mathematics is the stage.

And when two essays from the same year sit side by side—say Deng’s kinetic bridges and Wang’s tube geometry in 2026, or Smirnov’s percolation and Villani’s Landau damping in 2010—notice how different “modern mathematics” can sound while still sharing one spirit: **take a hard structure seriously until it yields a theorem**.
