**Fractals** are shapes with structure at every scale—coastlines that refuse to settle on a single length, Cantor sets that are uncountable yet measure zero, Julia sets whose boundary complexity lives in the complex plane, and the Mandelbrot set whose silhouette became a cultural icon of modern mathematics. The deep idea is that **roughness can be a geometric quantity**: dimension need not be an integer, and “pathological” sets are often the natural output of simple iterative rules.

**Path:** self-similarity and iterative constructions → Cantor set → fractal dimension (box-counting and Hausdorff) → complex dynamics and Julia/Mandelbrot → natural and applied fractals → confusions, exercises, further directions.

---

## Learning objectives

After this lecture you should be able to:

- Explain **self-similarity** and construct simple iterative fractals (middle-third Cantor set, Koch curve).
- Distinguish **topological dimension** from **fractal (Hausdorff/Minkowski) dimension** at an intuitive level.
- Compute box-counting dimension in elementary scaling examples (e.g. Koch curve, Sierpiński gasket).
- Describe the maps $$z\mapsto z^2+c$$ and the roles of Julia sets and the Mandelbrot set.
- Connect fractal dimension to real phenomena (coastlines, porous media) without overclaiming exact formulas.
- Avoid “fractal means pretty picture” and “dimension 1.5 means halfway between line and plane” as sole explanations.

**Prerequisites.** Sequences and limits; complex numbers at the level of $$z=x+iy$$; comfort with exponents and logarithms for dimension formulas. Measure theory is not required.

## 1. Structure at every scale

Ordinary geometry is scale-blind in a particular way: a smooth curve, zoomed enough, looks almost straight; a smooth surface looks almost flat. Fractals refuse that simplification. Zooming in reveals new detail that statistically or exactly resembles the whole. That property is **self-similarity**—exact for mathematical constructions, approximate for many natural forms.

A coastline measured with a short ruler is longer than the same coastline measured with a long ruler, because finer wiggles are included. Richardson and later Mandelbrot made this observation precise enough to ask: *what quantity captures how length scales with measurement resolution?* Fractal dimension answers that question for a wide class of sets.

---

## 2. The middle-third Cantor set

Start with the unit interval $$C_0=[0,1]$$. Remove the open middle third $$(1/3,2/3)$$ to get $$C_1=[0,1/3]\cup[2/3,1]$$. At each stage, remove open middle thirds of every remaining closed interval. The **Cantor set** is the intersection

$$
C=\bigcap_{n=0}^\infty C_n.
$$

Properties that reward careful thought:

- $$C$$ is uncountable (it can be identified with infinite sequences of left/right choices, hence with binary expansions—almost a continuum).
- $$C$$ has Lebesgue measure zero: the total length removed is $$1/3+2/9+4/27+\cdots=1$$.
- $$C$$ is closed, bounded, totally disconnected, and perfect (every point is a limit point).

So: **large in the sense of cardinality, empty in the sense of length**. Fractals live in the gap between different notions of size—the same theme that appears in Kakeya theory and geometric measure theory.

At stage $$n$$ one has $$2^n$$ intervals of length $$3^{-n}$$. That scaling foreshadows dimension $$\log 2/\log 3$$.

---

## 3. Koch curve and Sierpiński gasket

**Koch curve.** Start with a segment. Replace the middle third by the other two sides of an equilateral triangle. Iterate forever. Perimeter length after $$n$$ steps scales like $$(4/3)^n$$ times the original length and diverges as $$n\to\infty$$, while the curve remains a continuous image of an interval—infinite length in a bounded region of the plane.

**Sierpiński gasket.** Start with a filled equilateral triangle. Remove the open middle quarter triangle; iterate on the three remaining closed triangles. Area tends to zero; a residual fractal dust of positive dimension remains.

These constructions are **iterated function systems**: unions of scaled copies of themselves. If a set is the disjoint (or mildly overlapping) union of $$N$$ copies scaled by factor $$r$$, a candidate dimension $$d$$ solves

$$
N\, r^{d} = 1 \implies d = \frac{\log N}{\log(1/r)}.
$$

For the Cantor set, $$N=2$$, $$r=1/3$$, so $$d=\log 2/\log 3\approx 0.6309$$. For the Koch curve, $$N=4$$, $$r=1/3$$, so $$d=\log 4/\log 3\approx 1.2619$$. For the Sierpiński gasket, $$N=3$$, $$r=1/2$$, so $$d=\log 3/\log 2\approx 1.585$$.

## 4. Dimension: boxes, coverings, and intuition

**Minkowski (box-counting) dimension.** Cover a bounded set $$E\subset\mathbb{R}^n$$ by a grid of boxes of side length $$\varepsilon$$. Let $$N(\varepsilon)$$ be the number of boxes that meet $$E$$. If

$$
N(\varepsilon)\sim C\,\varepsilon^{-d}
$$

as $$\varepsilon\to 0$$, then $$d$$ is the box-counting dimension (when the limit of $$\log N(\varepsilon)/\log(1/\varepsilon)$$ exists). Roughly: how fast does the number of boxes explode as the grid refines?

**Hausdorff dimension** is a more refined measure-theoretic cousin based on coverings by sets of small diameter, not necessarily cubes. For many self-similar sets the two dimensions agree; for wilder sets they can differ. The important conceptual point for this course is that **dimension is a scaling exponent**, not merely a count of coordinates.

A set of Hausdorff dimension $$1.5$$ is “thicker than a curve, thinner than a region” in a precise scaling sense—not a blend of a line and a plane in ordinary geometric language. Dimension need not be integer; it need not match topological dimension; and measure zero does not force dimension zero (Cantor set) nor dimension less than the ambient space (Kakeya sets in the plane can have measure zero and dimension 2).

## 5. Complex dynamics: Julia sets and the Mandelbrot set

Fix a complex parameter $$c$$ and iterate

$$
z_{n+1} = z_n^2 + c,\qquad z_0\in\mathbb{C}.
$$

The **filled Julia set** for $$c$$ is the set of starting points $$z_0$$ whose orbit remains bounded; the **Julia set** is typically its boundary (for polynomials, the boundary of the filled Julia set). Depending on $$c$$, Julia sets can be connected and ornate, or totally disconnected “dust.”

The **Mandelbrot set** $$M$$ is the set of parameters $$c$$ for which the critical orbit starting at $$z_0=0$$ remains bounded:

$$
M=\bigl\{c\in\mathbb{C}:\text{the sequence }0,\,c,\,c^2+c,\,(c^2+c)^2+c,\,\ldots\text{ stays bounded}\bigr\}.
$$

Visually, $$M$$ is the black “cardioid with bulbs” of countless posters. Mathematically, connectivity of the Julia set for $$c$$ is linked to membership of $$c$$ in $$M$$ (Douady–Hubbard). Simple quadratic iteration generates infinite visual and topological complexity: **local rules, global roughness**.

Complex dynamics is a meeting point of geometry, analysis, and computation. Computer pictures are not mere decoration; they are experiments that suggested theorems about connectivity, hyperbolic components, and the structure of parameter space.

## 6. Fractals in nature and applications

Coastlines, clouds, river networks, bronchial trees, and porous materials often exhibit approximate scale invariance over limited ranges. Fractional dimensions appear in models of diffusion on rough media, antenna design, image compression, and financial volatility models (with varying degrees of empirical success). The mathematical virtue of fractal geometry is not that every natural form is a perfect self-similar set, but that **scaling exponents** become legitimate geometric invariants—tools to compare roughness across scales.

Mandelbrot’s slogan that “clouds are not spheres” is a methodological claim: if your geometric vocabulary only contains smooth manifolds, you will misdescribe nature. Fractals expand the vocabulary.

A careful applied stance distinguishes **scale ranges**. A real coastline may look fractal between meters and kilometers yet smooth at molecular scales and meaningless as a pure continuum beyond continental outlines. Mathematical fractals are infinite-zoom ideals; natural “fractals” are approximate over windows. The ideal still earns its keep: it supplies the scaling language that finite-range roughness needs.

---

## 7. Why it is beautiful

Fractals show that sets once dismissed as pathological—nowhere continuous derivatives, measure-zero uncountable sets, infinite-length curves—are **natural outputs of simple rules**. Beauty here is the marriage of elementary iteration with deep dimension theory, and the visual surprise that a quadratic map on the complex plane hides an entire universe of shapes. “Pathology” becomes “geometry of roughness.”

## 8. Common confusions

1. **“Fractal means self-similar and pretty.”** — Self-similarity is common but not the definition; dimension and multiscale structure matter more than aesthetics.
2. **“Dimension 1.5 means half line, half plane.”** — It is a scaling exponent, not a mixture of geometric types.
3. **“The Mandelbrot set is a Julia set.”** — Mandelbrot lives in *parameter* space; Julia sets live in *dynamical* space for fixed $$c$$.
4. **“Infinite length is a contradiction.”** — Infinite one-dimensional measure in a bounded region is compatible with continuity; think of highly oscillatory limits.
5. **“Measure zero means dimension zero.”** — Cantor set and many fractals refute this.
6. **“Natural fractals equal exact IFS copies.”** — Nature is approximate and scale-limited; mathematical fractals are idealizations.

## Exercises

1. Compute the first three stages of the middle-third Cantor construction and the total length removed after three stages.
2. Derive $$d=\log 2/\log 3$$ for the Cantor set from the self-similarity relation $$2\cdot(1/3)^d=1$$.
3. Explain why the Koch curve can have infinite length while remaining a continuous image of $$[0,1]$$.
4. For the Sierpiński gasket, justify $$d=\log 3/\log 2$$ and compare with topological dimension $$1$$.
5. In ≤200 words, contrast box-counting dimension with ordinary Euclidean dimension of a smooth curve.
6. Pick a parameter $$c$$ on the real axis (e.g. $$c=-1$$, $$c=1/4$$, $$c=-2$$) and discuss boundedness of the critical orbit qualitatively.
7. (Stretch) Read a short exposition of Hausdorff measure and state one way it differs from box counting.
