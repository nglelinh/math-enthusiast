**Symmetry** is sameness under transformation. A snowflake looks the same after a $$60^\circ$$ rotation; a circle looks the same after any rotation; the algebraic identity $$(a+b)^2=a^2+2ab+b^2$$ is unchanged if you swap $$a$$ and $$b$$. Group theory is the language that turns these observations into precise structure. The beauty of symmetry is not only aesthetic: **classifying symmetries often classifies the objects themselves**, and the more symmetry you demand, the more rigid—and often the more elegant—the outcome.

**Path:** transformations and invariants → groups as symmetry algebras → examples in geometry and algebra → wallpaper and crystals → continuous symmetry and conservation → Galois idea → confusions, exercises, further directions.

Symmetry is also a *selection principle*. When a problem has many candidate solutions, imposing symmetry often isolates the ones that nature or mathematics prefers: circular drops, hexagonal honeycombs, highly symmetric error-correcting codes, and packing configurations that attain record densities. The same principle that makes a snowflake pretty makes an existence proof shorter.

## Learning objectives

After this lecture you should be able to:

- Define a **symmetry** of an object as a structure-preserving invertible transformation.
- State the **group axioms** and recognize the symmetry set of a figure as a group under composition.
- Compute small symmetry groups (rectangle, equilateral triangle, square) and distinguish cyclic from dihedral behavior.
- Explain why there are only **seventeen wallpaper groups** as a classification fact (not a full proof).
- Connect continuous symmetries to the *idea* of conservation laws (Noether’s principle at slogan level).
- Avoid “symmetry means left-right reflection only” and “groups are only permutation tables.”

**Prerequisites.** Functions, composition, inverse functions; basic plane geometry. Linear algebra helps for rotations and reflections as matrices but is not mandatory.

## 1. What symmetry means mathematically

Informally, a symmetry is a way to move an object so that it looks unchanged. Formally, fix a mathematical object $$X$$ with structure (a set of points, a metric, a coloring, an equation, a crystal lattice). A **symmetry** of $$X$$ is an invertible map $$f:X\to X$$ that preserves the structure: distances, incidence, algebraic relations, or whatever the object cares about.

Examples:

- Isometries of the plane that map a polygon to itself.
- Permutations of roots of a polynomial that preserve all polynomial relations with rational coefficients (Galois).
- Linear automorphisms of a vector space that preserve an inner product (orthogonal group).
- Relabelings of vertices of a graph that preserve adjacency (graph automorphisms).

The empty slogan “symmetry is beauty” becomes operational: **beauty is invariance under a rich group of transformations**.

---

## 2. Groups: the algebra of composition

If $$f$$ and $$g$$ are symmetries of $$X$$, so is the composition $$f\circ g$$. The identity map is always a symmetry. Every symmetry has an inverse symmetry. Composition is associative. These four facts are the **group axioms**.

A **group** is a set $$G$$ with an operation $$\cdot$$ such that:

1. **Closure:** $$a,b\in G\implies a\cdot b\in G$$.
2. **Associativity:** $$(a\cdot b)\cdot c=a\cdot(b\cdot c)$$.
3. **Identity:** there exists $$e$$ with $$e\cdot a=a\cdot e=a$$.
4. **Inverses:** for each $$a$$ there exists $$a^{-1}$$ with $$a\cdot a^{-1}=a^{-1}\cdot a=e$$.

Symmetry groups need not be commutative: rotating then reflecting can differ from reflecting then rotating. The non-commutativity is not a pathology; it is geometric information.

## 3. Finite geometric examples

**Equilateral triangle.** Rotations by $$0^\circ,120^\circ,240^\circ$$ and three reflections form the **dihedral group** $$D_3$$ of order $$6$$ (sometimes written $$D_6$$ depending on convention—check order, not name). Labeling vertices $$1,2,3$$ realizes the group as all permutations of three letters: $$D_3\cong S_3$$.

**Square.** Eight symmetries: rotations by multiples of $$90^\circ$$ and four reflections (axes through opposite vertices or midpoints of opposite sides). This is $$D_4$$, order $$8$$.

**Rectangle that is not a square.** Only four symmetries: identity, $$180^\circ$$ rotation, and two reflections through midlines. The reduced symmetry group *detects* that adjacent sides are unequal.

**Circle.** Infinitely many rotations and reflections: the orthogonal group $$O(2)$$ in two dimensions. Continuous families of symmetries appear.

These examples illustrate a theme: **to know the object, know its symmetry group**—or at least let the group constrain what the object can be.

A useful classroom check is to draw a figure, list candidate motions, and test each motion against the figure’s distinguishing features (side lengths, angles, colors). What remains is the symmetry group. Students who skip the test often overcount by including motions that *almost* work—rotating a non-square rectangle by $$90^\circ$$ is the classic trap.

### Cayley’s slogan (optional depth)

Abstract groups are not only geometric. Cayley’s theorem says every finite group is isomorphic to a subgroup of a symmetric group $$S_n$$: symmetries of *something* (a finite set of labels). Geometry supplies the intuition; permutation representations supply the algebra. The two views reinforce each other throughout modern mathematics.

## 4. Wallpaper patterns and crystallographic restriction

A **wallpaper pattern** is a repeating design in the plane: invariant under two linearly independent translations, and possibly reflections, rotations, and glide reflections. Remarkably, there are exactly **seventeen** distinct wallpaper groups up to affine equivalence of symmetry type—a classical theorem of plane crystallography.

Not every rotational order is allowed for a lattice-periodic pattern. The **crystallographic restriction** implies that periodic plane patterns can have rotational symmetries only of orders $$1,2,3,4,6$$—not order $$5$$, for example. Fivefold rotation is compatible with *aperiodic* order (Penrose tilings, quasicrystals) but not with ordinary periodic wallpaper. Symmetry classification thus predicts which visual rhythms can tile the plane by repetition.

Three-dimensional crystallography expands the story: **230 space groups** classify symmetries of crystal lattices. Chemistry and materials science speak group theory whether or not they use the words.

The wallpaper count “seventeen” is a theorem, not a design tip. It says that once you fix the discrete isometry type of a doubly periodic pattern, only seventeen inequivalent combinations of rotations, reflections, glides, and translations occur. Artists rediscover these types endlessly; mathematicians prove the list is complete. That completeness is itself a form of beauty: the plane’s rigid motions are constrained enough that infinite-looking variety collapses to a short catalogue.

## 5. Continuous symmetry and conservation (idea level)

In physics, continuous symmetries of the laws of motion are linked to conservation laws by **Noether’s theorem**: time-translation symmetry relates to energy conservation; spatial translation to momentum; rotation to angular momentum. The precise statement needs Lagrangian mechanics; the conceptual message for this course is enough:

> **Invariance under a continuous family of transformations is not decorative—it forces a conserved quantity.**

In pure mathematics, continuous symmetry groups are often **Lie groups**—groups that are also smooth manifolds, such as $$SO(3)$$ (rotations in 3D) or $$U(n)$$ (unitary matrices). Representation theory studies how such groups act linearly on vector spaces, organizing spectra, particles, and special functions.

## 6. Symmetry in algebra: a Galois sketch

Consider a polynomial equation with rational coefficients. Its roots may require radicals or more exotic extensions of $$\mathbb{Q}$$. **Galois theory** associates a group of automorphisms of the splitting field that fix the base field; the structure of that group governs which quantities are expressible by radicals.

The unsolvability of the general quintic by radicals is a statement about the symmetric group $$S_5$$ (and alternating $$A_5$$) not having the composition-series shape that radical extensions require. Symmetry of roots becomes an obstruction to formulas. Again: **classifying symmetry classifies possibility**.

Even without the full machinery, one can appreciate the shift of perspective: instead of hunting clever substitutions, one asks which permutations of roots could arise from field automorphisms, then reads off what radical towers can achieve. The group is a compressed certificate of impossibility or possibility—exactly the spirit of modern algebra.

## 7. Why mathematicians care

- **Classification.** Finite simple groups; regular polyhedra; wallpaper and space groups; compact Lie groups—symmetry organizes zoos of objects into short lists.
- **Reduction.** If a problem is symmetric, solutions can often be sought in invariant subspaces or reduced variables.
- **Rigidity.** High symmetry often implies uniqueness (up to congruence) of geometric objects—Platonic solids, highly symmetric graphs, extremal configurations.
- **Communication between fields.** The same group can appear in geometry, number theory, and physics, forming bridges (a theme that reappears in Langlands-style programs at a much higher altitude).

Beautiful mathematics here is the discovery that “looking the same” is an algebraic structure with theorems, classifications, and impossibilities.

## 8. Common confusions

1. **“Symmetry means bilateral reflection.”** — Rotations, translations, glides, and algebraic automorphisms count.
2. **“A group is a set of numbers.”** — Groups are sets with operations; elements can be functions, matrices, or geometric motions.
3. **“More symmetry always means more possibilities.”** — Often the opposite: symmetry constraints *reduce* free parameters.
4. **“Wallpaper groups are about wallpaper glue.”** — They classify discrete isometry groups of the plane with a lattice of translations.
5. **“Noether’s theorem says every symmetry conserves something in every system.”** — It applies in precise variational settings; the slogan needs hypotheses.
6. **“Quasicrystals contradict crystallography.”** — They expand the notion of order beyond periodicity; fivefold motifs appear without lattice translation symmetry.

## Exercises

1. List all symmetries of a non-square rectangle and verify the group has four elements.
2. Show that the symmetries of an equilateral triangle form a group of order $$6$$ by labeling vertices.
3. Give an example of two symmetries whose composition depends on order (non-commutative).
4. Why can’t a periodic wallpaper pattern have fivefold rotational symmetry? (Give the restriction statement; a full lattice proof is optional.)
5. In ≤200 words, explain how a symmetry group can classify an object.
6. Identify one continuous symmetry of the circle and one discrete symmetry of a regular hexagon.
7. (Stretch) Look up the statement that there are $$17$$ wallpaper groups and name three types (e.g. pure translations; p4m; p6m).
8. (Stretch) Write three sentences connecting Galois groups to “symmetries of roots.”
