Walk into the Alhambra palace in Granada and tilt your head up at the ceramic tilework. At first glance, the walls look like an endless riot of geometric invention—stars interlocking with hexagons, ribbons weaving into pinwheels, reflections folding into rotations. Yet a mathematician looking at the same walls sees something startling: across every repeating two-dimensional pattern ever drawn by human hands or grown in a flat crystal, there are **exactly seventeen** distinct ways symmetry can be arranged—not sixteen, not eighteen, and not infinitely many.

That is the hidden superpower of **symmetry**. Everyday language treats symmetry as a pretty mirror reflection, something belonging to art class or butterfly wings. In mathematics, symmetry is *sameness under transformation*, and **group theory** is the algebraic engine that captures it. Once you turn "looking the same" into an algebraic structure, symmetry ceases to be decorative: **classifying symmetries classifies the objects themselves**, predicts which crystal structures can exist in nature, explains why physical quantities like energy and momentum are conserved, and even proves why there is no quadratic-like formula for fifth-degree polynomial equations.

**Path:** transformations and invariants → groups as symmetry algebras → concrete matrix computations in $$D_3$$ → wallpaper groups and the crystallographic restriction → continuous symmetry and conservation → Galois theory → common confusions and further directions.

## Learning objectives

After this lecture you should be able to:

- Define a **symmetry** of an object as a structure-preserving invertible transformation.
- State the **group axioms** and recognize the symmetry set of a figure as a group under composition.
- Compute small symmetry groups (rectangle, equilateral triangle, square) using permutations or $$2\times 2$$ matrices, and distinguish commutative from non-commutative behavior.
- Explain why there are only **seventeen wallpaper groups** and prove why a periodic lattice cannot have 5-fold rotational symmetry.
- Connect continuous symmetries to conservation laws via **Noether’s theorem** at the conceptual level.
- Avoid confusing symmetry with left-right reflection alone, or thinking groups are merely sets of numbers.

**Prerequisites.** Functions, composition, inverse functions, and basic plane geometry. Elementary $$2\times 2$$ matrix multiplication makes the worked examples come alive.

## Key ideas: What Is Group Theory and Why Does It Unify Mathematics?

If you graduated in science, engineering, or economics, you have probably met symmetry informally—even functions $$f(-x)=f(x)$$, symmetric matrices $$A^T=A$$, or balanced equations. Modern mathematics elevates that intuition into four interlocking pillars:

1. **Abstract Algebra & Group Theory:** Instead of studying a geometric shape directly, you study the *collection of all structure-preserving transformations* acting on it, equipped with composition as an operation. Amazingly, completely different worlds—rotations of a triangle, shuffles of three cards, and permutations of the roots of a cubic polynomial—can have the exact same group structure (*isomorphism*).
2. **Discrete Geometry & Crystallography:** When symmetry acts on a repeating lattice in 2D or 3D, rigid geometric constraints collapse infinite visual variety into finite catalogues: **17 wallpaper groups** in the plane and **230 space groups** in three dimensions. Materials scientists and chemists use this catalogue to classify every mineral crystal on Earth.
3. **Lie Groups & Theoretical Physics:** When symmetries vary continuously—like rotating a sphere by any angle $$\theta$$ or shifting an experiment forward in time by $$\Delta t$$—the symmetry group becomes a smooth curved space called a **Lie group**. Through **Emmy Noether’s theorem**, every continuous symmetry of a physical law forces a conservation law (time shift $$\to$$ energy; space shift $$\to$$ momentum; rotation $$\to$$ angular momentum).
4. **Galois Theory & Structural Obstructions:** Évariste Galois realized that the roots of a polynomial equation have their own hidden symmetries—permutations that preserve all rational algebraic relations among the roots. By analyzing the structure of this **Galois group**, you can prove *without ever trying to solve the equation* whether a formula using radicals exists.

## 1. What symmetry means mathematically

Informally, a symmetry is a way to move an object so that it lands back on itself looking unchanged. Formally, fix a mathematical object $$X$$ equipped with some structure (distances, incidence, a coloring, an equation, or a crystal lattice). A **symmetry** of $$X$$ is an invertible map $$f:X\to X$$ that preserves that structure.

Examples across mathematics:

- **Plane geometry:** Rigid motions (isometries) that map a polygon back onto itself.
- **Algebra (Galois):** Permutations of the roots of a polynomial that preserve all polynomial relations with rational coefficients.
- **Linear algebra:** Linear automorphisms of a vector space that preserve lengths and angles (the orthogonal group $$O(n)$$).
- **Graph theory:** Relabelings of vertices that preserve which pairs are connected by an edge (graph automorphisms).

The vague slogan "symmetry is beauty" becomes operational: **beauty is invariance under a rich group of transformations**. Moreover, symmetry acts as a *selection principle*: when a problem has countless candidate solutions, demanding symmetry often isolates the optimal ones—spherical soap bubbles, hexagonal honeycombs, and record-density sphere packings.

## 2. Groups: the algebra of composition

Notice what happens whenever you collect *all* symmetries of an object $$X$$:

- Doing one symmetry $$g$$ and then another symmetry $$f$$ produces a new symmetry $$f\circ g$$.
- Doing nothing at all (the identity map $$e$$) always preserves the object.
- Because every symmetry is invertible, you can always undo it ($$f^{-1}$$).
- Composing three transformations is automatically associative: $$(f\circ g)\circ h = f\circ(g\circ h)$$.

Abstracting these four properties gives the definition of a **group**: a set $$G$$ together with a binary operation $$\cdot$$ satisfying:

1. **Closure:** $$a,b\in G \implies a\cdot b\in G$$.
2. **Associativity:** $$(a\cdot b)\cdot c = a\cdot(b\cdot c)$$ for all $$a,b,c\in G$$.
3. **Identity:** There exists $$e\in G$$ such that $$e\cdot a = a\cdot e = a$$ for all $$a\in G$$.
4. **Inverses:** For each $$a\in G$$, there exists $$a^{-1}\in G$$ such that $$a\cdot a^{-1} = a^{-1}\cdot a = e$$.

Notice what is *not* on the list: commutativity. Rotating a shape and then reflecting it generally gives a different result from reflecting first and rotating second. That non-commutativity is not a flaw—it is geometric information encoded directly into algebra.

## 3. Finite geometric examples—and a hands-on matrix computation

Let us test the group axioms on familiar polygons:

- **Equilateral triangle ($$D_3$$, order 6):** Three rotations ($$0^\circ, 120^\circ, 240^\circ$$) and three reflections across the medians. Labeling the vertices $$1, 2, 3$$ identifies $$D_3$$ with the symmetric group $$S_3$$ of all $$3!=6$$ permutations.
- **Square ($$D_4$$, order 8):** Four rotations (multiples of $$90^\circ$$) and four reflections (two diagonals, two midlines).
- **Non-square rectangle (order 4):** Only the identity, a $$180^\circ$$ rotation, and two midline reflections. The smaller symmetry group *detects* that adjacent sides are unequal—rotating by $$90^\circ$$ fails to land the rectangle back on itself.
- **Circle ($$O(2)$$, infinite):** Infinitely many rotations by any angle $$\theta \in [0,2\pi)$$ and infinitely many reflections across diameters.

### Worked example 1: Computing non-commutativity in $$D_3$$ with $$2\times 2$$ matrices

Place an equilateral triangle centered at the origin with one vertex on the positive $$x$$-axis. Two symmetries generate the entire group $$D_3$$:
- $$R$$: counterclockwise rotation by $$120^\circ$$, represented by the matrix
  $$
  R = \begin{pmatrix} \cos 120^\circ & -\sin 120^\circ \\ \sin 120^\circ & \cos 120^\circ \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -\tfrac{1}{2} & -\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \\ \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2} & -\tfrac{1}{2} \end{pmatrix}.
  $$
- $$S$$: reflection across the $$x$$-axis, represented by
  $$
  S = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}.
  $$

Now multiply them in both orders (remembering that $$SR$$ means "apply $$R$$ first, then $$S$$"):

$$
SR = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} -\tfrac{1}{2} & -\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \\ \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2} & -\tfrac{1}{2} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -\tfrac{1}{2} & -\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \\ -\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2} & \tfrac{1}{2} \end{pmatrix},
$$

$$
RS = \begin{pmatrix} -\tfrac{1}{2} & -\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \\ \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2} & -\tfrac{1}{2} \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -\tfrac{1}{2} & \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \\ \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2} & \tfrac{1}{2} \end{pmatrix}.
$$

Look at the off-diagonal entries: $$SR \neq RS$$! Even better, check that $$R^2 = \begin{pmatrix} -\tfrac{1}{2} & \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \\ -\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2} & -\tfrac{1}{2} \end{pmatrix}$$, and verify by multiplying that $$SR = R^2 S$$. That single algebraic relation—$$SR = R^2S$$ together with $$R^3 = I$$ and $$S^2 = I$$—completely defines the entire 6-element symmetry group of the triangle without ever needing to draw a picture again.

### Cayley’s slogan (optional depth)

Abstract groups are not limited to polygons. **Cayley’s theorem** guarantees that every finite group is isomorphic to a subgroup of a symmetric group $$S_n$$—that is, symmetries of *some* finite set of labels. Geometry supplies the visual intuition; permutations and matrices supply the computational engine.

## 4. Wallpaper patterns and the crystallographic restriction

A **wallpaper pattern** is a repeating design in the plane that is invariant under two linearly independent translations (forming a 2D **lattice**), and possibly also under rotations, reflections, and glide reflections. Remarkably, a classical theorem of plane crystallography proves that there are **exactly seventeen** distinct wallpaper groups. In three dimensions, the analogous classification yields **230 space groups**—the complete periodic table of crystal symmetries.

Why can’t a repeating wallpaper or ordinary crystal have 5-fold ($$72^\circ$$) rotational symmetry? You do not have to take this on faith—you can prove it in five lines of linear algebra.

### Worked example 2: Proving the crystallographic restriction with matrix traces

Suppose a rotation by angle $$\theta$$ maps a 2D lattice back onto itself. In a basis formed by two lattice generators $$\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2$$, the rotation sends every lattice vector to an integer combination of $$\mathbf{v}_1$$ and $$\mathbf{v}_2$$. Therefore, in this lattice basis, the rotation is represented by a $$2\times 2$$ **integer matrix** $$M$$.

Because the **trace** (sum of diagonal entries) of a matrix is invariant under change of basis, the trace of $$M$$ must equal the trace of the standard Cartesian rotation matrix:

$$
\operatorname{tr}(M) = \cos\theta + \cos\theta = 2\cos\theta.
$$

Since $$M$$ has integer entries, $$2\cos\theta$$ **must be an integer**! And since $$-1 \le \cos\theta \le 1$$, the only possible values for $$2\cos\theta$$ are $$-2, -1, 0, 1, 2$$:

| $$2\cos\theta$$ | $$\cos\theta$$ | Rotation angle $$\theta$$ | Rotational order $$n = 360^\circ/\theta$$ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $$2$$ | $$1$$ | $$0^\circ$$ (or $$360^\circ$$) | $$1$$ |
| $$1$$ | $$1/2$$ | $$60^\circ$$ | $$6$$ |
| $$0$$ | $$0$$ | $$90^\circ$$ | $$4$$ |
| $$-1$$ | $$-1/2$$ | $$120^\circ$$ | $$3$$ |
| $$-2$$ | $$-1$$ | $$180^\circ$$ | $$2$$ |

What about a 5-fold rotation ($$\theta = 72^\circ$$)? Here $$2\cos 72^\circ = \frac{\sqrt{5}-1}{2} \approx 0.618$$, which is **not an integer**—so no periodic 2D lattice can ever possess 5-fold rotational symmetry!

To verify that order 6 *does* work, consider the hexagonal lattice generated by $$\mathbf{v}_1 = (1,0)$$ and $$\mathbf{v}_2 = (\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2})$$. Rotating by $$60^\circ$$ sends $$\mathbf{v}_1 \mapsto \mathbf{v}_2$$ and $$\mathbf{v}_2 \mapsto \mathbf{v}_2 - \mathbf{v}_1$$, giving the exact integer matrix

$$
M = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix},
$$

whose trace is $$0 + 1 = 1 = 2\cos 60^\circ$$ and whose sixth power is $$M^6 = I$$. Fivefold symmetry is not impossible in nature—it appears in **Penrose tilings** and **quasicrystals**—but those structures must give up strict lattice periodicity.

## 5. Continuous symmetry and conservation (idea level)

In physics, continuous symmetries of the laws of motion are linked to conservation laws by **Noether’s theorem** (1918):

- If the laws of physics do not change from yesterday to tomorrow (**time-translation symmetry**), **energy** is conserved.
- If the laws are the same here and ten meters away (**spatial-translation symmetry**), **linear momentum** is conserved.
- If the laws do not care which direction your laboratory faces (**rotational symmetry**), **angular momentum** is conserved.

The full proof uses Lagrangian mechanics and the calculus of variations, but the conceptual headline is unforgettable:

> **Invariance under a continuous family of transformations is not decorative—it forces a conserved quantity.**

In pure mathematics, continuous symmetry groups are **Lie groups**—groups that are also smooth manifolds, such as $$SO(3)$$ (3D rotations) or $$U(n)$$ (unitary matrices). Representation theory studies how these groups act linearly on vector spaces, organizing atomic spectra, elementary particles, and special functions.

## 6. Symmetry in algebra: a Galois sketch

Symmetry is not restricted to space. Consider the simple equation

$$
x^2 - 2 = 0,
$$

whose roots are $$\sqrt{2}$$ and $$-\sqrt{2}$$. From the viewpoint of rational numbers $$\mathbb{Q}$$, these two roots are completely indistinguishable: every algebraic equation with rational coefficients that holds for $$\sqrt{2}$$ also holds for $$-\sqrt{2}$$. The map $$\sigma(a + b\sqrt{2}) = a - b\sqrt{2}$$ swaps the two roots while leaving every rational number fixed—it is an algebraic symmetry! Together with the identity map, it forms a 2-element **Galois group** isomorphic to $$\mathbb{Z}_2$$.

For a general polynomial of degree $$n$$, **Galois theory** attaches a finite group of root permutations that preserve all rational relations among the roots. Being solvable by a radical formula (using $$+, -, \times, \div$$ and $$k$$-th roots) corresponds precisely to the Galois group being decomposable into simple commutative layers (a *solvable group*). For degree 5, the general Galois group is $$S_5$$, whose core subgroup $$A_5$$ (order 60) is **simple and non-commutative**—it cannot be broken down further. That single structural fact proves no general quintic radical formula can exist. Again: **classifying symmetry classifies what is possible**.

### Explanation: From "Looking the Same" to Proving Impossibility

How does a child’s intuition about mirror-symmetric butterflies grow into a machine that rules out 5-fold crystals and quintic formulas? Walk through the four-step logic slowly:

1. **Shift focus from the object to its transformations.** Instead of staring at a triangle, a crystal lattice, or a polynomial equation, list every invertible transformation that leaves its essential structure intact.
2. **Capture the interaction of transformations as a group.** Notice that composing two symmetries yields another symmetry, and encode the rules of composition in a group $$G$$ (via permutations, matrices, or relations like $$SR = R^2S$$).
3. **Extract an invariant that bridges two worlds.** For a crystal lattice, the matrix trace $$\operatorname{tr}(M) = 2\cos\theta$$ bridges *geometry* (rotation angle) and *arithmetic* (integers). For a polynomial, the composition series of the Galois group bridges *field extensions* (radicals) and *group structure* (solvability).
4. **Read off rigidity or impossibility directly from the algebra.** Once $$2\cos\theta \in \mathbb{Z}$$ fails for $$\theta=72^\circ$$, or once $$A_5$$ refuses to decompose into abelian layers, no amount of clever geometry or algebraic trial-and-error can override the obstruction.

**Quick check.** Using the trace test $$2\cos\theta \in \mathbb{Z}$$, explain in one sentence why an 8-fold rotation ($$\theta = 45^\circ$$) is also impossible in a periodic 2D wallpaper pattern. *(Hint: compute $$2\cos 45^\circ = \sqrt{2}$$.)*

## 7. Why mathematicians care

- **Classification.** Finite simple groups, regular polyhedra, wallpaper and space groups, compact Lie groups—symmetry organizes vast zoos of objects into clean catalogues.
- **Reduction.** If a problem is symmetric, solutions can often be sought in invariant subspaces or reduced variables, dramatically cutting computational complexity.
- **Rigidity.** High symmetry often implies uniqueness up to congruence—Platonic solids, extremal graphs, and record sphere packings.
- **Bridges between fields.** The exact same group can govern a crystal, a differential equation, and a number-theoretic extension—a theme that echoes all the way to the [Langlands program]({{ site.baseurl }}/contents/en/chapter02/02_02_Langlands_Program/).

Beautiful mathematics here is the discovery that "looking the same" is an algebraic engine with theorems, classifications, and ironclad impossibilities.

## 8. Common confusions

1. **“Symmetry means bilateral reflection.”** — Rotations, translations, glide reflections, and algebraic automorphisms all count.
2. **“A group is a set of numbers.”** — Groups are sets with operations; elements are often geometric motions, matrices, or permutations.
3. **“More symmetry always means more possibilities.”** — Usually the opposite: symmetry constraints *reduce* free parameters and enforce rigidity.
4. **“Wallpaper groups are about wallpaper glue.”** — They classify discrete isometry groups of the plane containing a 2D lattice of translations.
5. **“Noether’s theorem says every symmetry conserves something in every system.”** — It applies in variational (Lagrangian) settings to continuous symmetries; discrete symmetries work differently.
6. **“Quasicrystals contradict crystallography.”** — They expand the notion of order beyond lattice periodicity; fivefold motifs appear without periodic translation symmetry.
