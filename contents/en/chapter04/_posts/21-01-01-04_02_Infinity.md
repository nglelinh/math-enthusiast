Picture yourself standing at the front desk of a grand hotel with infinitely many rooms numbered $$1, 2, 3, \ldots$$—and every single room is occupied. Late at night, a weary traveler walks in asking for a bed. Moments later, an infinitely long tour bus pulls up outside with infinitely many more guests. In any finite hotel, the clerk simply apologizes. In the world of **infinity**, a few clever room-reassignment announcements fit every single newcomer inside without turning anyone away.

For more than two millennia after Aristotle, mathematicians hesitated to treat infinity as a finished object for fear of paradox. Then in the late 19th century, Georg Cantor did something audacious: he put infinite sets on a balance scale to **compare their sizes**. The shock was not that infinity is huge, but that **infinity comes in strictly different sizes**—an endless ladder with no top rung, and with questions about its rungs that escape the standard axioms of mathematics itself.

As the **flagship lesson of Chapter 4**, this essay develops the machinery carefully enough to compare infinite sizes with confidence and spot the subtle traps that appear when infinity enters calculus and probability.

**Path:** potential vs actual infinity → countable infinity and Hilbert’s hotel → uncountable continuum → hierarchy and continuum hypothesis → infinity in analysis vs set theory → ordinals as a different infinite → confusions and further directions.

## Learning objectives

After this lecture you should be able to:

- Distinguish **potential infinity** (unending process) from **actual infinity** (completed infinite set).
- Explain why $$\lvert\mathbb{N}\rvert = \lvert\mathbb{Z}\rvert = \lvert\mathbb{Q}\rvert$$ via explicit pairings (countable infinity).
- Sketch **Cantor’s diagonal argument** that $$\lvert\mathbb{R}\rvert > \lvert\mathbb{N}\rvert$$.
- State **Cantor’s theorem** on power sets and the resulting hierarchy of infinities.
- State the **continuum hypothesis** as a question, not a theorem, and note its independence from ZFC.
- Compare “infinite” in calculus (limits, series) with cardinal infinity in set theory.
- Avoid “infinity is a number you can do arithmetic with like 7.”
- Separate **almost-sure eventual occurrence** from a usable waiting time (infinite monkeys), and say why a doubling-down betting rule is an analysis warning, not a house-beating theorem.

**Prerequisites.** Comfort with functions and sets at a naive level. Diagonal details pair with Section 5’s proof-idea essay on Cantor’s argument. No formal axiomatic set theory is required, but willingness to treat infinite sets as mathematical objects is essential.

## Background Knowledge Map: What Is Infinite Set Theory and What Role Does It Play Today?

In high school and introductory university calculus, the symbol $$\infty$$ almost always appears in limits like $$\lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{1}{n} = 0$$. It is tempting to imagine infinity as a "gigantic number" sitting at the far end of the number line. In modern mathematics, however, the word "infinity" wears **three completely different hats**, belonging to three distinct fields:

1. **Set Theory — Cardinality (Size):** Asks *"how many elements?"* via one-to-one pairings (bijections), ignoring order. This is the foundation of modern mathematics (ZFC set theory, infinite-dimensional function spaces) and computer science (separating countable lists of programs from uncountable real numbers).
2. **Order Theory — Ordinals (Arrangement):** Asks *"in what well-ordered pattern are the elements lined up?"* An infinite line followed by one last person ($$\omega + 1$$) has a completely different order type from an infinite line with no last person ($$\omega$$), even though both have the same cardinality! Ordinals power transfinite induction and termination proofs in logic.
3. **Analysis — Limiting Processes:** Asks *"what happens as a step count grows without bound?"* Here $$\infty$$ is shorthand for unbounded growth, serving as a safety harness for series, integrals, and probability.

The core mathematical move of this lesson is classic: **when finite intuition ("the whole is always larger than a proper part") shatters against infinity, we do not give up—we return to the most primitive definition of comparison (one-to-one pairing) and follow it rigorously to the end.**

## 1. Two faces of the infinite

**Potential infinity.** The process of counting $$1,2,3,\ldots$$ never ends. There is always a next integer. Greek mathematics often treated infinity this way: as an unending *procedure*, not a finished object. Aristotle’s distinction between the infinite as *potential* and the infinite as *actual* shaped centuries of caution.

**Actual infinity.** The set of all natural numbers

$$
\mathbb{N} = \{0,1,2,3,\ldots\}
$$

(or $$\{1,2,3,\ldots\}$$, convention-dependent) is treated as a completed totality—a single mathematical object you can quantify over, map, and compare. Once you accept $$\mathbb{N}$$ as a set, you can form $$\mathbb{Z}$$, $$\mathbb{Q}$$, $$\mathbb{R}$$, power sets, function spaces, and the whole landscape of modern analysis and algebra.

Modern mathematics uses both languages. Calculus speaks of limits “as $$n\to\infty$$” (process language). Set theory speaks of $$\lvert\mathbb{N}\rvert$$ (object language). Confusing them produces pseudo-paradoxes: people ask whether “infinity plus one” is larger than infinity without specifying whether they mean a process, a cardinal, or an ordinal. Precision dissolves the fog.

## 2. Countable infinity: same size as $$\mathbb{N}$$

Imagine checking whether a theater has as many seats as audience members without knowing how to count: simply ask everyone to sit down. If every person gets one seat and no seats remain empty, the two sets match in size. Cantor turned that exact idea into a definition: two sets have the same **cardinality** if there is a **bijection** (a perfect one-to-one pairing) between them. For infinite sets, this produces startling equalities:

- **Even numbers.** The map $$n \mapsto 2n$$ pairs $$0\leftrightarrow 0,\; 1\leftrightarrow 2,\; 2\leftrightarrow 4,\; 3\leftrightarrow 6,\ldots$$ A proper subset of an infinite set can match the whole set’s size.
- **Integers.** Interleave positives and negatives as $$0, 1, -1, 2, -2, 3, -3, \ldots$$ via $$0\mapsto 0$$, odd $$2k-1 \mapsto k$$, even $$2k \mapsto -k$$. Thus $$\lvert\mathbb{Z}\rvert=\lvert\mathbb{N}\rvert$$.
- **Rationals and a hand-verifiable Cantor pairing formula.** How do we assign every pair of positive integers $$(p, q)$$ its own unique room number in Hilbert's Hotel? Use **Cantor's pairing function**:
  $$\pi(p, q) = \frac{(p+q-2)(p+q-1)}{2} + p.$$
  Plug in the first few diagonals $$p+q = 2, 3, 4$$ by hand:
  - $$p+q=2$$: $$\pi(1,1) = 0 + 1 = \mathbf{1}$$ (fraction $$1/1$$)
  - $$p+q=3$$: $$\pi(1,2) = 1 + 1 = \mathbf{2}$$ ($$1/2$$), $$\pi(2,1) = 1 + 2 = \mathbf{3}$$ ($$2/1$$)
  - $$p+q=4$$: $$\pi(1,3) = 3 + 1 = \mathbf{4}$$ ($$1/3$$), $$\pi(2,2) = 3 + 2 = \mathbf{5}$$ ($$2/2$$), $$\pi(3,1) = 3 + 3 = \mathbf{6}$$ ($$3/1$$)

  Every pair $$(p, q)$$ lands in a unique positive integer room (for example, $$3/4$$ gets room $$\pi(3,4) = \frac{5\cdot 6}{2} + 3 = 18$$)! Skipping unreduced duplicates and interleaving $$0$$ and negatives shows $$\mathbb{Q}$$ is completely countable. **"Dense on the line" does not mean "larger infinity."**

![Hilbert hotel]({{ site.baseurl }}/img/chapter_img/infinity_hilbert_hotel.svg)

*Figure. Hilbert’s hotel: a full countable hotel can still free a room by re-indexing.*

**Hilbert’s hotel** dramatizes countable infinity: if every room $$1,2,3,\ldots$$ is full, move guest $$n$$ to room $$n+1$$; room 1 opens. You can absorb countably many new guests by sending $$n\mapsto 2n$$ and filling the odds. The algebra of infinite cardinals is not the algebra of finite counting: for the smallest infinite cardinal $$\aleph_0$$,

$$
\aleph_0 + 1 = \aleph_0,\qquad \aleph_0 + \aleph_0 = \aleph_0,\qquad \aleph_0\cdot\aleph_0 = \aleph_0.
$$

## 3. Uncountable infinity: the continuum

After watching both $$\mathbb{Z}$$ and $$\mathbb{Q}$$ check smoothly into Hilbert's Hotel, you might guess that *every* infinite set has size $$\aleph_0$$. In 1874 (and with his famous diagonal proof in 1891), Cantor proved the opposite: the real numbers are **uncountable**.

Let's walk through the **diagonal argument** on $$(0,1)$$ with a concrete table:

1. Suppose (for contradiction) someone hands you a complete list $$r_1, r_2, r_3, \ldots$$ of *all* real numbers in $$(0,1)$$ written in decimal form:
   - $$r_1 = 0.\mathbf{3}14159\ldots$$
   - $$r_2 = 0.2\mathbf{7}1828\ldots$$
   - $$r_3 = 0.50\mathbf{0}000\ldots$$
   - $$r_4 = 0.141\mathbf{5}92\ldots$$
   - $$r_5 = 0.8888\mathbf{8}8\ldots$$
   - $$\dots$$
2. Read down the **main diagonal** (bold digits: $$3, 7, 0, 5, 8, \ldots$$) and build a brand-new number $$d$$ by a simple rule: if the $$n$$th digit of $$r_n$$ is $$5$$, write $$6$$; otherwise write $$5$$. From the table above, we immediately get:
   $$d = 0.55565\ldots$$
3. Check every row directly: $$d$$ differs from $$r_1$$ in the 1st digit ($$5 \neq 3$$), from $$r_2$$ in the 2nd ($$5 \neq 7$$), from $$r_3$$ in the 3rd ($$5 \neq 0$$), from $$r_4$$ in the 4th ($$6 \neq 5$$), from $$r_5$$ in the 5th ($$5 \neq 8$$), and in general from $$r_n$$ in the $$n$$th place. So $$d \in (0,1)$$ **cannot appear anywhere on the list**. Contradiction!

Hence no countable list can hold all real numbers: $$\lvert\mathbb{R}\rvert > \lvert\mathbb{N}\rvert$$.

![Cardinal comparison]({{ site.baseurl }}/img/chapter_img/infinity_cardinal_compare.svg)

*Figure. Countable infinity vs continuum size (schematic).*

**Technical footnote (honesty).** Decimal representations are not unique ($$0.1999\ldots=0.2000\ldots$$). Choosing only digits $$5$$ and $$6$$ automatically dodges both trailing $$0$$s and trailing $$9$$s, so $$d$$ cannot secretly equal a listed number under an alternate decimal expansion.

The same diagonal *pattern* reappears across logic and computer science: undecidability of Turing's halting problem, existence of non-computable reals, and Gödel's incompleteness theorem. Diagonalization is not a trick about decimals; it is a universal engine for escaping any enumeration.

## 4. A hierarchy of infinities

Are there only two sizes of infinity? Not at all. **Cantor's theorem** states that for *every* set $$A$$, its power set $$\mathcal{P}(A)$$ (the collection of all subsets of $$A$$) is strictly larger:

$$
\lvert A\rvert < \lvert\mathcal{P}(A)\rvert.
$$

**Hand-verifiable worked example of the diagonal set $$D = \{x \in A : x \notin f(x)\}$$:**
Take the tiny finite set $$A = \{1, 2, 3\}$$ (size $$3$$, while $$\mathcal{P}(A)$$ has $$2^3 = 8$$ subsets) and test a concrete candidate map $$f: A \to \mathcal{P}(A)$$:
- $$f(1) = \{1, 2\}$$ — is $$1 \in f(1)$$? **Yes** $$\implies$$ leave $$1$$ out of $$D$$.
- $$f(2) = \{2, 3\}$$ — is $$2 \in f(2)$$? **Yes** $$\implies$$ leave $$2$$ out of $$D$$.
- $$f(3) = \{1\}$$ — is $$3 \in f(3)$$? **No** $$\implies$$ put $$3$$ into $$D$$.

This yields $$D = \{3\}$$. Glance back at the image of $$f$$: $$\{1,2\},\ \{2,3\},\ \{1\}$$—sure enough, $$D = \{3\}$$ was **missed**! Now let $$A$$ be *any* infinite set: if someone claimed $$D = f(a)$$ for some $$a \in A$$, we immediately hit the contradiction $$a \in D \iff a \notin f(a) = D$$. Starting from $$\mathbb{N}$$, we climb an endless ladder:

$$
\lvert\mathbb{N}\rvert < \lvert\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})\rvert < \lvert\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N}))\rvert < \cdots
$$

There is **no largest infinity**. The continuum $$\lvert\mathbb{R}\rvert$$ equals $$\lvert\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})\rvert$$ (each subset of $$\mathbb{N}$$ corresponds to an infinite binary $$0/1$$ sequence, i.e., a binary expansion in $$[0,1]$$).

**Continuum hypothesis (CH).** Is there a cardinality strictly between $$\lvert\mathbb{N}\rvert$$ and $$\lvert\mathbb{R}\rvert$$? Kurt Gödel (1940) and Paul Cohen (1963) proved a landmark result: CH is **independent** of the standard ZFC axioms of set theory—neither provable nor disprovable from them, much like Euclid's parallel postulate. Infinity is not just large; it is **foundationally subtle**.

### Deep-Dive Explanation: From Hilbert's Hotel to the Independence of CH (Read Slowly)

To keep all dialects of infinity cleanly separated, walk slowly through the four-step spine of this lesson:

1. **Lock in "same size" via bijections:** Without a last element to count to, we define $$\lvert A\rvert = \lvert B\rvert$$ by one-to-one pairing. That puts $$\mathbb{N}, \mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Q}$$ on the exact same rung $$\aleph_0$$ (Hilbert's Hotel).
2. **Escape every list via diagonalization:** Against any countable list of reals in $$(0,1)$$, flipping the $$n$$th digit of the $$n$$th entry manufactures a real number missing from the list. Hence $$\lvert\mathbb{R}\rvert > \lvert\mathbb{N}\rvert$$.
3. **Generalize to an infinite ladder:** The exact same diagonal trick ($$D = \{x: x\notin f(x)\}$$) proves $$\lvert A\rvert < \lvert\mathcal{P}(A)\rvert$$ for every set $$A$$, showing there is no largest infinity.
4. **Hit the boundary of the axioms (CH):** Asking whether any size sits strictly between $$\lvert\mathbb{N}\rvert$$ and $$\lvert\mathbb{R}\rvert$$ runs into a foundational limit—ZFC leaves the question genuinely open (independent).

**Quick check.** If someone asks "Is $$\infty + 1$$ bigger than $$\infty$$?", the mathematically fluent reply is: *"Which dialect do you mean—cardinals ($$\aleph_0 + 1 = \aleph_0$$), ordinals ($$\omega + 1 > \omega$$), or a calculus limit?"*

## 5. Infinity in calculus is a different dialect

In calculus:

- $$\lim_{n\to\infty} a_n = L$$ means: for every $$\varepsilon>0$$ there exists $$N$$ such that for all $$n\ge N$$, $$\lvert a_n-L\rvert<\varepsilon$$.
- The symbol $$\infty$$ in $$\lim_{x\to\infty}$$ is not a real number; it abbreviates unbounded growth of the independent variable.
- Extended real numbers $$+\infty$$ and $$-\infty$$ are sometimes added for bookkeeping of unbounded limits, but they are not reals and do not obey ordinary arithmetic.

You can have

$$
\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{1}{n^2} = \frac{\pi^2}{6} < \infty
$$

(a finite sum of infinitely many positive terms—the series converges) while

$$
\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{1}{n} = \infty
$$

(divergence of the harmonic series—grouping terms by powers of 2 shows $$\frac{1}{3}+\frac{1}{4} > \frac{1}{2}$$, $$\frac{1}{5}+\cdots+\frac{1}{8} > \frac{1}{2}$$, pushing the sum past any bound). **Infinite processes** can converge or diverge; that is analysis. **Infinite sets** have cardinalities; that is set theory. Both use the word “infinite.” They answer different questions.

### Almost surely is not a timetable (after Tao)

In a [Big Think conversation](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OOMx2BHHWtE) for the forthcoming *Six Math Essentials*, Terence Tao treats analysis as the seatbelt you put on infinity. Two classroom cartoons from that talk belong beside the calculus dialect above. The course hub is [Six Math Essentials]({{ site.baseurl }}/contents/en/chapter04/04_01_Six_Math_Essentials/).

The **infinite-monkeys** theorem says that a monkey typing forever—or infinitely many monkeys typing—will, **almost surely**, eventually produce any fixed finite text, *Hamlet* included. The mechanism is ordinary probability: if each block of keystrokes has a positive chance $$p > 0$$ of matching the target, independent retries drive the chance $$(1-p)^N$$ of *never* matching to zero. The expected waiting time $$1/p$$, however, grows exponentially with length: on a 26-letter keyboard, a 4-letter word averages $$26^4 \approx 456{,}976$$ tries, whereas a 100-letter passage averages $$26^{100} \approx 3 \times 10^{141}$$ tries—dwarfing the number of atoms in the observable universe. “Probability one in the limit” is a different claim from “soon enough to watch.”

A second cartoon is the **doubling-down** (martingale) rule on an even-money bet: bet $$1$$ dollar, then $$2, 4, 8, 16, \ldots$$ after losses so that one win recovers everything plus $$1$$ dollar. The catch, as Tao stresses, is an **infinite bankroll**. After just $$10$$ straight losses (chance $$1/1024$$), you are down $$1023$$ dollars and must wager $$1024$$ dollars on round 11 just to chase $$1$$ dollar of profit. With finite money, the strategy merely compresses ruin into a tiny event where stakes are astronomical. Analysis lets you first imagine infinite resources—Tao likens it to a video-game infinite-health code—and then return, carefully, to the finite world.

Neither story replaces Cantor’s hierarchy. They warn that the *analytic* dialect of infinity (limits, almost-sure events, idealized capital) has its own traps, distinct from “how many reals are there?”

## 6. Ordinals: infinity as order type

Cardinals measure size. **Ordinals** measure well-ordered order types. After all finite numbers $$0, 1, 2, \ldots$$ come the first infinite ordinal $$\omega$$, then $$\omega+1$$, $$\omega+2$$, $$\omega\cdot 2$$, $$\omega^2$$, $$\omega^\omega$$, and far beyond. The key point for this course is conceptual: **ordinal infinity remembers order**, while **cardinal infinity forgets order and remembers only bijection**.

Compare $$1 + \omega$$ and $$\omega + 1$$ directly:
- In $$1 + \omega$$, placing one extra element at the **front** of the line gives $$(*, 0, 1, 2, 3, \ldots)$$, which has the exact same order structure (no last element), so $$1 + \omega = \omega$$.
- In $$\omega + 1$$, placing one extra element at the **end** gives $$(0, 1, 2, 3, \ldots, *)$$, which now has a **last element** sitting after an infinite initial segment! Thus $$\omega + 1 \neq \omega$$ (ordinal addition is not commutative!).

Yet as *sets*, both $$\omega$$ and $$\omega+1$$ are countable—same cardinal $$\aleph_0$$. Hilbert’s hotel is about cardinals; transfinite induction lives in the ordinal world.

## 7. Why mathematicians care

- **Foundations.** What objects exist? What infinite constructions are legitimate? Which axioms settle which questions?
- **Analysis and topology.** Completeness of $$\mathbb{R}$$, compactness arguments (open covers and finite subcovers), infinite-dimensional function spaces, and measure theory all rest on completed infinities.
- **Computer science.** Countable vs uncountable languages; non-computable reals; diagonalization in complexity and logic; the distinction between “enumerable in principle” and “decidable by an algorithm.”
- **Culture of rigor.** Infinity is where informal talk most easily lies; precise definitions pay rent. Beauty here is not “pretty pictures” alone—it is the shock that **infinite can mean more than one size**, and that some size questions escape ordinary axioms.

## 8. Common confusions

1. **“$$\infty$$ is the biggest number.”** — Not a real number; cardinals form a hierarchy with no maximum.
2. **“There are more fractions than integers.”** — Both countable; density is not cardinality.
3. **“Diagonalization is just a trick about decimals.”** — It is a general method of escape from enumerations, reused across logic and computability.
4. **“CH must be true or false like RH.”** — CH is independent of ZFC; the Riemann hypothesis is a definite arithmetic statement about zeros of a complex function (widely believed to have a truth value in the standard integers).
5. **“Unbounded sequence = completed infinite set.”** — Process language vs object language.
6. **“$$\omega+1$$ is a larger infinity than $$\omega$$.”** — Larger as ordinals (order type), not as cardinals (size).
7. **“Almost surely means it happens this week.”** — Infinite monkeys: probability one is not a waiting-time bound.
8. **“Doubling bets beat the house.”** — Only with infinite capital; finite ruin is the hidden event.
