**Infinity** is not one thing. It is a family of ideas about unbounded processes, completed totalities, and—after Cantor—**different sizes** of infinite sets. This **Section 4 flagship** develops those ideas carefully enough to support seminar discussion and LO2-style comparisons of “size.” The shock is not that infinity is large; the shock is that “infinite” can mean more than one size, and that some size questions escape the ordinary axioms of set theory.

**Path:** potential vs actual infinity → countable infinity and Hilbert’s hotel → uncountable continuum → hierarchy and continuum hypothesis → infinity in analysis vs set theory → ordinals as a different infinite → confusions, exercises, and further directions.

---

## Learning objectives

After this lecture you should be able to:

- Distinguish **potential infinity** (unending process) from **actual infinity** (completed infinite set).
- Explain why $$\lvert\mathbb{N}\rvert = \lvert\mathbb{Z}\rvert = \lvert\mathbb{Q}\rvert$$ via explicit pairings (countable infinity).
- Sketch **Cantor’s diagonal argument** that $$\lvert\mathbb{R}\rvert > \lvert\mathbb{N}\rvert$$.
- State **Cantor’s theorem** on power sets and the resulting hierarchy of infinities.
- State the **continuum hypothesis** as a question, not a theorem, and note its independence from ZFC.
- Compare “infinite” in calculus (limits, series) with cardinal infinity in set theory.
- Avoid “infinity is a number you can do arithmetic with like 7.”

**Prerequisites.** Comfort with functions and sets at a naive level. Diagonal details pair with Section 5’s proof-idea essay on Cantor’s argument. No formal axiomatic set theory is required, but willingness to treat infinite sets as mathematical objects is essential.

## 1. Two faces of the infinite

**Potential infinity.** The process of counting $$1,2,3,\ldots$$ never ends. There is always a next integer. Greek mathematics often treated infinity this way: as an unending *procedure*, not a finished object. Aristotle’s distinction between the infinite as *potential* and the infinite as *actual* shaped centuries of caution. One could accept that a process goes on without bound without admitting a completed infinite collection as a legitimate object of thought.

**Actual infinity.** The set of all natural numbers

$$
\mathbb{N} = \{0,1,2,3,\ldots\}
$$

(or $$\{1,2,3,\ldots\}$$, convention-dependent) is treated as a completed totality—a single mathematical object you can quantify over, map, and compare. Once you accept $$\mathbb{N}$$ as a set, you can form $$\mathbb{Z}$$, $$\mathbb{Q}$$, $$\mathbb{R}$$, power sets, function spaces, and the whole landscape of modern analysis and algebra.

Modern mathematics uses both languages. Calculus speaks of limits “as $$n\to\infty$$” (process language). Set theory speaks of $$\lvert\mathbb{N}\rvert$$ (object language). Confusing them produces pseudo-paradoxes: people ask whether “infinity plus one” is larger than infinity without specifying whether they mean a process, a cardinal, or an ordinal. Precision dissolves the fog.

## 2. Countable infinity: same size as $$\mathbb{N}$$

Two sets have the same **cardinality** if there is a bijection between them—a perfect pairing. For finite sets this matches ordinary counting. For infinite sets it becomes a definition of “same size,” and the definition yields surprises.

Surprising samenesses:

- **Even numbers.** The map $$n \mapsto 2n$$ pairs $$\mathbb{N}$$ with $$\{0,2,4,\ldots\}$$. A proper subset of an infinite set can match the whole set’s size. Galileo already noticed this tension for squares; modern set theory elevates it to a definition rather than a paradox.
- **Integers.** List $$0,1,-1,2,-2,3,-3,\ldots$$. So $$\lvert\mathbb{Z}\rvert=\lvert\mathbb{N}\rvert$$. The negatives do not create a “larger” infinity; they can be interleaved with the positives.
- **Rationals.** $$\mathbb{Q}$$ is countable: arrange nonnegative fractions in a grid (numerator and denominator) and snake through, skipping duplicates and reducing fractions. A similar construction handles negative rationals. “Dense on the line” does not mean “larger infinity.” Every open interval of reals contains infinitely many rationals, yet the full set of rationals is no larger, in the cardinal sense, than the integers.

![Hilbert hotel]({{ site.baseurl }}/img/chapter_img/infinity_hilbert_hotel.svg)

*Figure. Hilbert’s hotel: a full countable hotel can still free a room by re-indexing.*

**Hilbert’s hotel** dramatizes countable infinity: if every room $$1,2,3,\ldots$$ is full, move guest $$n$$ to room $$n+1$$; room 1 opens. You can absorb countably many new guests by sending $$n\mapsto 2n$$ and filling the odds. You can even accommodate countably many coaches of countably many guests by a careful enumeration of pairs. The algebra of infinite cardinals is not the algebra of finite counting: for the smallest infinite cardinal $$\aleph_0$$,

$$
\aleph_0 + 1 = \aleph_0,\qquad \aleph_0 + \aleph_0 = \aleph_0,\qquad \aleph_0\cdot\aleph_0 = \aleph_0.
$$

## 3. Uncountable infinity: the continuum

Cantor proved that the real numbers are **not** countable. One standard argument is the **diagonal argument** on $$(0,1)$$:

1. Suppose (for contradiction) there is a complete list $$r_1,r_2,r_3,\ldots$$ of reals in $$(0,1)$$, each written as an infinite decimal.
2. Build a number $$d$$ whose $$n$$th decimal digit differs from the $$n$$th digit of $$r_n$$ (for example, choose digit $$4$$ if the $$n$$th digit of $$r_n$$ is not $$4$$, and digit $$5$$ otherwise).
3. Then $$d$$ is in $$(0,1)$$ but equals no $$r_n$$, because it differs from $$r_n$$ in the $$n$$th place. Contradiction.

Hence no listing of all reals exists: $$\lvert\mathbb{R}\rvert > \lvert\mathbb{N}\rvert$$.

![Cardinal comparison]({{ site.baseurl }}/img/chapter_img/infinity_cardinal_compare.svg)

*Figure. Countable infinity vs continuum size (schematic).*

**Technical footnote (honesty).** Decimal representations are not unique ($$0.1999\ldots=0.2000\ldots$$). A careful proof uses a digit-avoiding rule that dodges the dual expansions, or works with binary expansions under a fixed convention. The *idea*—diagonal escape from any purported complete list—is what you must own for this course.

The same diagonal *pattern* reappears in logic and computability: undecidability of the halting problem, non-enumerability of the set of all computable reals, and Gödel-style constructions that build a sentence “disagreeing with the $$n$$th theory on the $$n$$th claim.” Diagonalization is not a trick about decimals; it is a general method of escape from enumerations.

## 4. A hierarchy of infinities

Cantor’s theorem: for any set $$A$$, the power set $$\mathcal{P}(A)$$ (the set of all subsets of $$A$$) is strictly larger in cardinality:

$$
\lvert A\rvert < \lvert\mathcal{P}(A)\rvert.
$$

The proof is itself diagonal: if $$f:A\to\mathcal{P}(A)$$ were surjective, the set $$D=\{x\in A:x\notin f(x)\}$$ would lie outside the image of $$f$$. Starting from $$\mathbb{N}$$,

$$
\lvert\mathbb{N}\rvert < \lvert\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})\rvert < \lvert\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N}))\rvert < \cdots
$$

There is **no largest infinity**. The continuum $$\lvert\mathbb{R}\rvert$$ equals $$\lvert\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})\rvert$$ under standard identifications (reals coded by binary expansions or characteristic functions of subsets of $$\mathbb{N}$$, carefully handled for non-uniqueness). Thus the reals sit strictly above the naturals, and the set of all sets of reals sits strictly above the reals, and so on.

**Continuum hypothesis (CH).** Is there a cardinality strictly between $$\lvert\mathbb{N}\rvert$$ and $$\lvert\mathbb{R}\rvert$$? In symbols: does every infinite subset of $$\mathbb{R}$$ inject into $$\mathbb{N}$$ or biject with $$\mathbb{R}$$? Gödel (consistency of CH with ZFC, via the constructible universe) and Cohen (independence via forcing) showed that CH is **independent** of the standard ZFC axioms: neither provable nor disprovable from those axioms alone. Infinity becomes not only big, but **foundationally subtle**. Different models of set theory can answer CH differently without contradicting the usual axioms.

## 5. Infinity in calculus is a different dialect

In calculus:

- $$\lim_{n\to\infty} a_n = L$$ means: for every $$\varepsilon>0$$ there exists $$N$$ such that for all $$n\ge N$$, $$\lvert a_n-L\rvert<\varepsilon$$.
- The symbol $$\infty$$ in $$\lim_{x\to\infty}$$ is not a real number; it abbreviates unbounded growth of the independent variable.
- Extended real numbers $$+\infty$$ and $$-\infty$$ are sometimes added for bookkeeping of unbounded limits, but they are not reals and do not obey ordinary arithmetic.

You can have

$$
\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{1}{n^2} < \infty
$$

(a finite sum of infinitely many positive terms—the series converges) while

$$
\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{1}{n} = \infty
$$

(divergence of the harmonic series). **Infinite processes** can converge or diverge; that is analysis. **Infinite sets** have cardinalities; that is set theory. Both use the word “infinite.” They answer different questions. Conflating “the series goes on forever” with “the set of terms has cardinality larger than $$\mathbb{N}$$” is a category error: the index set of a series is already countable by definition.

## 6. Ordinals: infinity as order type

Cardinals measure size. **Ordinals** measure well-ordered order types. After all finite numbers come the first infinite ordinal $$\omega$$, then $$\omega+1$$, $$\omega+2$$, $$\omega\cdot 2$$, $$\omega^2$$, $$\omega^\omega$$, and far beyond. The key point for this course is conceptual: **ordinal infinity remembers order**, while **cardinal infinity forgets order and remembers only bijection**.

In particular, $$\omega$$ and $$\omega+1$$ are different ordinals (one has a last element after an infinite initial segment; the other is pure successor-free infinity at the top), yet as *sets* they are both countable—same cardinal $$\aleph_0$$. Hilbert’s hotel is about cardinals; induction and recursion on well-ordered structures live in the ordinal world. Keeping the two dialects separate prevents many textbook confusions about “infinity plus one.”

## 7. Why mathematicians care

- **Foundations.** What objects exist? What infinite constructions are legitimate? Which axioms settle which questions?
- **Analysis and topology.** Completeness of $$\mathbb{R}$$, compactness arguments (open covers and finite subcovers), infinite-dimensional function spaces, and measure theory all rest on completed infinities.
- **Computer science.** Countable vs uncountable languages; non-computable reals; diagonalization in complexity and logic; the distinction between “enumerable in principle” and “decidable by an algorithm.”
- **Culture of rigor.** Infinity is where informal talk most easily lies; precise definitions pay rent. Beauty here is not “pretty pictures” alone—it is the shock that **infinite can mean more than one size**, and that some size questions escape ordinary axioms.

## 8. Common confusions

1. **“$$\infty$$ is the biggest number.”** — Not a real number; cardinals form a hierarchy with no maximum.
2. **“There are more fractions than integers.”** — Both countable; density is not cardinality.
3. **“Diagonalization is just a trick about decimals.”** — It is a general method of escape from enumerations, reused across logic and computability.
4. **“CH must be true or false like RH.”** — CH is independent of ZFC; the Riemann hypothesis is a definite arithmetic statement about zeros of a complex function (widely believed to have a truth value in the standard integers).
5. **“Unbounded sequence = completed infinite set.”** — Process language vs object language.
6. **“$$\omega+1$$ is a larger infinity than $$\omega$$.”** — Larger as ordinals (order type), not as cardinals (size).
