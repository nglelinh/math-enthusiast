**Tilings** cover the plane without gaps or overlaps—by polygons, by curved tiles, periodically or not. Honeycombs, bathrooms, Islamic geometric art, and crystal lattices are tiling cultures. Mathematics asks: which shapes tile? Must a tiling repeat? Can a single tile force aperiodicity? Local rules generate global order—or enforce **never-repeating** order. Tilings connect art, crystallography, group theory, and logic (undecidability of the general tiling problem).

**Path:** regular and Archimedean tessellations → symmetry and wallpaper → aperiodic tilings and Penrose → monotiles (einstein problem) → undecidability → confusions, exercises, further directions.

Tilings also offer a clean laboratory for the chapter’s broader theme: simple local constraints can force global phenomena that no single tile “knows about.” A hexagon does not contain the word “honeycomb,” yet honeycomb order is what equal hexagonal tiles produce. An aperiodic monotile does not contain the word “non-periodic,” yet non-periodicity is what its geometry enforces. Beauty and logic share the same mechanism—constraint propagation across the plane.

## 1. What is a tiling?

A **tiling** (tessellation) of the plane is a covering by tiles (compact sets, often polygons) whose interiors are disjoint and whose union is the whole plane. Tiles may be congruent to one another (monohedral tiling) or drawn from a finite set of prototiles.

**Edge-to-edge** polygonal tilings require edges to meet full edge to full edge. Relaxing rules allows wilder constructions, but the classical theory already contains deep theorems.

---

## 2. Regular and semiregular tessellations

A **regular tessellation** uses copies of a single regular polygon, same arrangement at every vertex. Interior angle constraints imply only three:

- equilateral triangles (six at a vertex: $$6\times 60^\circ=360^\circ$$),
- squares (four at a vertex),
- regular hexagons (three at a vertex).

Regular pentagons fail: $$108^\circ$$ does not divide $$360^\circ$$ in an edge-to-edge regular way that fills the plane. (Pentagons reappear in *aperiodic* and non-regular stories.)

**Archimedean (semiregular) tessellations** allow more than one type of regular polygon but require vertex-transitive arrangements—finitely many classical types decorate textbooks and architecture.

---

## 3. Periodicity and wallpaper symmetry

A tiling is **periodic** if it is invariant under two linearly independent translations—a lattice of repeats. Periodic tilings are classified in part by their symmetry groups, linking to the **seventeen wallpaper groups**. Honeycombs are hexagonal periodic order; brickwork is rectangular; some patterns include reflections and glides.

Periodicity is the mathematical idealization of a crystal’s repeating unit cell. Crystallography’s dictionary is tiling theory plus group theory.

---

## 4. Aperiodic order: Penrose tilings

Can tiles cover the plane **only** non-periodically? Wang tiles and Berger’s work showed that aperiodic prototile sets exist (1960s): there are finite tile sets that tile the plane, but **every** tiling they admit fails to be periodic.

**Penrose tilings** (1970s) give a beautiful geometric example: rhombs or kite/dart pairs with matching rules that force aperiodicity and produce local fivefold symmetry motifs without global translational periodicity. Hierarchical inflation/deflation rules explain self-similarity: zoom out, and the same matching patterns reappear at larger scales.

Quasicrystals (Shechtman et al., 1980s) showed that nature can realize ordered non-periodic structures with forbidden crystallographic symmetries—Penrose-like order entered materials science. Tiling theory predicted aesthetic possibilities that physics later met.

Matching rules are the quiet heroes. Without them, two rhombs might form periodic patterns; with them, every infinite tiling is forced into hierarchical non-periodicity. Local edge labels or vertex figures act like a grammar: only certain words (tilings) are grammatical, and none of the grammatical words is periodic. That is a profound computational idea wearing geometric clothing—closely related, in spirit, to the later undecidability results.

---

## 5. The einstein problem and monotiles

Is there a **single** tile (an “einstein,” German for “one stone”) that tiles the plane only aperiodically? For decades this was open.

In 2023, **David Smith**, with **Myers, Kaplan, and Goodman-Strauss**, announced a continuum of aperiodic monotiles culminating in widely discussed **hat** and **spectre** polygons: monohedral tilings of the plane that admit no periodic tiling. Reflections were involved in early hat tilings; spectre variants refine chirality conditions. The precise history is moving quickly; the conceptual victory is stable:

> **A single shape can force global non-periodicity.**

Local geometry constrains global order so strongly that translational lattice symmetry becomes impossible.

---

## 6. Logic: tiling is undecidable

**Wang’s conjecture** hoped that any tile set that can tile the plane can do so periodically. Berger disproved it and, more dramatically, showed that there is **no algorithm** that decides, given an arbitrary finite set of Wang tiles, whether they tile the plane. The tiling problem is **undecidable**.

The proof reduces from problems about Turing machines: clever tile sets can simulate computation so that tiling exists if and only if a machine never halts (or a related condition). Geometry encodes logic. This is one of the great bridges between discrete geometry and computability—cousin to incompleteness and undecidability themes elsewhere in the course.

---

## 7. Why mathematicians care

- **Classification.** Which polygons tile monohedrally? (Still rich open questions historically; many cases settled, some subtle.)
- **Order without period.** Mathematical model of quasicrystals and hierarchical structures.
- **Symmetry.** Wallpaper groups; crystallographic restriction.
- **Computation.** Undecidability as a geometric theorem.
- **Art.** Constraints become design languages.

Beautiful mathematics here is the spectrum from bathroom tile simplicity to logical incompleteness—same question: *what covers the plane?*

## 8. Common confusions

1. **“Non-periodic means disordered.”** — Penrose tilings are highly ordered, just not periodic.
2. **“Only three shapes tile.”** — Only three *regular* tessellations; countless irregular tiles work.
3. **“Aperiodic monotile means it never repeats any pattern.”** — Hierarchical repetitions occur; translational period does not.
4. **“Undecidable means we cannot study any tilings.”** — Specific families are decidable and classifiable; the *general* problem has no algorithm.
5. **“Fivefold symmetry is impossible.”** — Impossible for *periodic* crystals; possible for quasiperiodic order.
6. **“Hat tile is pure chaos.”** — Matching geometry is rigid and rule-based.

## Matching rules, Wang tiles, and computation

**Wang tiles** are unit squares with colored edges that may abut only when colors match. Berger proved that whether a finite Wang set tiles the plane is **undecidable**, and that there exist aperiodic Wang sets. The plane becomes a computing surface: local color rules can simulate a Turing machine’s tape evolution, so solvability of the tiling problem would solve the halting problem. That single transfer of difficulty is why “just check all small patches” cannot decide the general question.

For undergrad seminar purposes you need only the slogan: **geometry can encode logic**. When you meet later complexity-theory essays, remember that tiling already supplies a geometric NP-hard and undecidable family of decision problems.

## Hierarchical substitution and quasicrystals

Penrose and many aperiodic systems admit **inflation**: each tile subdivides into smaller copies of the same prototile set, scaled by a fixed factor. Iterating inflation produces hierarchical structure—order without lattice periodicity. Physical **quasicrystals** (Shechtman discovery; Nobel chemistry 2011) realize diffraction patterns with “forbidden” rotational symmetries; mathematical models use cut-and-project schemes from higher-dimensional lattices. Beauty here is empirical as well as formal: sharp diffraction peaks coexist with aperiodicity.

Compare two slogans carefully:

- **Periodic crystal:** discrete translation group of full rank; Bragg peaks on a lattice dual.
- **Quasicrystal / cut-and-project:** order from a higher lattice projected irrationally; still highly structured, not random.

## Monohedral aperiodicity and the hat–spectre arc

For decades it was open whether a **single** connected tile (einstein—“one stone”) can force aperiodicity. Smith–Myers–Kaplan–Goodman-Strauss (2023–) produced the **hat** monotile and related **spectre** variants that tile only aperiodically (with careful reflection conventions depending on the variant). Pedagogically the moral is twofold: (1) aperiodicity need not require a large tile inventory; (2) open questions about “simple” plane geometry can stay open for half a century and then resolve with computer-assisted case analysis plus human geometric insight.

When you sketch the hat, do not claim you have verified the global theorem. Claim only: *the community now has an explicit monotile story; the verification lives in published papers and community checks.*

## Seminar studio: build a rule, break a period

1. Design three edge colors and a set of four Wang-like squares.
2. Show that a period-2 checkerboard coloring of the plane is blocked by at least one local mismatch under your rules—or exhibit a period-2 tiling if you failed to block it.
3. Write two paragraphs: what local rule you intended, and what global consequence (if any) you can prove on a $$4\times4$$ torus.

This studio is not research; it is training for the local-to-global habit that aperiodic theory perfects.

## Why bathrooms still look periodic

Most manufactured tiles *are* periodic: manufacturing, cutting, and human visual preference favor lattices. Mathematics shows that **periodicity is optional**, not mandatory, once matching rules or shapes become sufficiently clever. The chapter’s beauty theme is not “everything pretty is periodic,” but “order has more species than wallpaper.”
