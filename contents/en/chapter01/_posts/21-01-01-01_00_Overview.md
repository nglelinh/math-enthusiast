Some mathematical questions are short enough to fit on the back of a postcard—and simple enough that a high-school student can understand the prompt in ten seconds. Yet decades, and sometimes more than a century, pass without a proof.

Pick any positive integer: if it is even, halve it; if it is odd, triple it and add one. Does that bouncing journey *always* fall back to 1? Stir a splash of cream into your morning coffee: can the swirling fluid governed by the Navier–Stokes equations ever focus its energy into a single point of infinite velocity in finite time? Or look at the prime numbers scattered along the number line like grains of sand: is there a hidden symphony of near-perfect regularity governing where they appear?

The real magic of great open problems is not the million-dollar prize money or the glory of crossing the finish line first. In the history of mathematics, every great problem acts like a lighthouse: on the voyage toward a solution, mathematicians are forced to invent entire new continents of theory. In this chapter, you will meet problems that remain undefeated, one that saw a once-in-a-century breakthrough in 2025, and one—the Four Color Theorem—that was solved in a way that ignited a lasting philosophical debate about what counts as a mathematical proof.

## Background Knowledge Map: Three Continents Behind Chapter 1

If you have an undergraduate background in calculus, linear algebra, and basic probability, terms like *complex analysis*, *algebraic geometry*, *partial differential equations*, or *gauge theory* may sound distant. In reality, all ten lessons in this chapter sit on **three major pillars** connected directly to undergraduate mathematics:

### 1. The Discrete & Arithmetic Pillar
This world studies countable structures—integers, prime numbers, rational solutions to polynomial equations, and graphs. Its driving question is: *beneath apparent discrete chaos, what hidden algebraic or statistical order exists?* The lessons here include the [Riemann Hypothesis]({{ site.baseurl }}/contents/en/chapter01/01_02_Riemann_Hypothesis/), [Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer]({{ site.baseurl }}/contents/en/chapter01/01_04_Birch_Swinnerton_Dyer/), [Twin Primes]({{ site.baseurl }}/contents/en/chapter01/01_06_Twin_Prime_Conjecture/), [Collatz]({{ site.baseurl }}/contents/en/chapter01/01_07_Collatz_Conjecture/), and the [Four Color Theorem]({{ site.baseurl }}/contents/en/chapter01/01_09_Four_Color_Theorem/). Real-world impact spans public-key cryptography (RSA, ECC), error-correcting codes, network routing, and compiler design.

### 2. The Continuous Analysis, Geometry & Mathematical Physics Pillar
This world studies fields evolving continuously across space and time—fluids, waves, quantum fields—along with subtle notions of geometric size beyond ordinary length, area, and volume. Its driving question is: *do the equations describing nature always admit smooth, unique, mathematically rigorous solutions?* The lessons here include [Navier–Stokes]({{ site.baseurl }}/contents/en/chapter01/01_05_Navier_Stokes/), [Kakeya]({{ site.baseurl }}/contents/en/chapter01/01_08_Kakeya_Conjecture/), and [Yang–Mills]({{ site.baseurl }}/contents/en/chapter01/01_10_Yang_Mills_Mass_Gap/). Applications range from aerodynamics, weather forecasting, and MRI imaging to the Standard Model of particle physics.

### 3. The Computation, Algorithms & Formal Proof Pillar
This world studies the fundamental limits of efficient algorithms and the role of computers in mathematical reasoning. Its driving question is: *is finding a solution genuinely harder than checking one, and how can machines verify proofs beyond human scale?* The lessons here include [P vs NP]({{ site.baseurl }}/contents/en/chapter01/01_03_P_vs_NP/), the [Four Color Theorem]({{ site.baseurl }}/contents/en/chapter01/01_09_Four_Color_Theorem/), and the optional 2022–2026 essay [Modern Applications]({{ site.baseurl }}/contents/en/chapter01/01_10_modern_applications/).

## Two Hands-On Mini-Experiments to Feel Why Great Problems Resist

Before diving into individual essays, try two quick calculations with pen and paper to feel the chasm between "understanding the question in ten seconds" and "proving it for infinitely many cases":

1. **Experiment 1 — Planar Maps and Euler's Inequality (Four Color Theorem):**
   Consider the complete graph $$K_4$$ with $$v = 4$$ vertices and $$e = \binom{4}{2} = 6$$ edges. You can draw it flat on paper with no crossing edges (a triangle with one vertex inside), and it clearly requires 4 colors. Now add just one vertex to form $$K_5$$ ($$v = 5,\, e = \binom{5}{2} = 10$$). Try drawing it flat: *at least two edges are forced to cross!* Why? Euler's formula $$v - e + f = 2$$ for simple planar graphs implies $$e \le 3v - 6$$. Plug in $$v = 5$$: $$3(5) - 6 = 9 < 10$$! One line rules out $$K_5$$—yet proving that *every* planar graph is 4-colorable took 124 years and hundreds of machine-checked configurations.
2. **Experiment 2 — Calm vs. Wild Orbits (Collatz):**
   Apply the rule *halve if even, $$3n+1$$ if odd*:
   - Starting at $$n = 6$$: $$6 \to 3 \to 10 \to 5 \to 16 \to 8 \to 4 \to 2 \to 1$$ (8 steps, peak 16).
   - Starting at $$n = 27$$: $$27 \to 82 \to 41 \to 124 \to 62 \to 31 \to \cdots$$ rockets up to a peak of **9,232** before finally landing on 1 after **111 steps**!

### Deep-Dive Explanation: Why Checking a Billion Cases Is Still Not a Proof (Read Slowly)

New readers naturally ask: *"Computers have checked trillions of Riemann zeros and over $$2^{71}$$ starting numbers for Collatz without a single failure—why won't mathematicians call it proved?"* Follow these four steps:

#### Step 1: Finite verification never reaches infinity
Checking $$10^{21}$$ cases leaves **infinitely many** cases unchecked. A universal theorem ("for every $$n \in \mathbb{N}$$") is an all-or-nothing claim: a single counterexample at $$n = 10^{1000}$$ destroys it completely.

#### Step 2: Mathematics has a famous history of giant-scale "false leads"
Gauss noticed that the prime-counting function $$\pi(x)$$ stayed below $$\operatorname{li}(x)$$ for every $$x$$ anyone could compute—and modern checks confirm $$\pi(x) < \operatorname{li}(x)$$ past $$10^{14}$$. Yet in 1914, John Littlewood proved that $$\pi(x) - \operatorname{li}(x)$$ **changes sign infinitely often**, with the first crossover hiding far beyond everyday computation (below $$1.4 \times 10^{316}$$)! Numerical evidence alone would have predicted the exact opposite.

#### Step 3: "Computer-assisted proof" vs. "computer testing"
In the [Four Color Theorem]({{ site.baseurl }}/contents/en/chapter01/01_09_Four_Color_Theorem/), computers *did* help prove the theorem—not by sampling millions of random maps, but because human theory (the discharging method) **compressed infinitely many planar graphs into a finite checklist of 633 configurations**, and the computer exhausted 100% of that finite list. For [Collatz]({{ site.baseurl }}/contents/en/chapter01/01_07_Collatz_Conjecture/) or [Riemann]({{ site.baseurl }}/contents/en/chapter01/01_02_Riemann_Hypothesis/), no one knows how to compress infinity into a finite checklist.

#### Step 4: A proof explains *why*, not just *whether*
A proof does more than stamp "True" on a conjecture—it exposes the hidden mechanism forcing the statement to hold, and the new tools invented along the way become the lasting legacy of the problem.

## How to Approach This Chapter

The goal here is not to solve a million-dollar problem, but to step inside the working workshop of modern mathematics:

- **Understand what is actually asked:** state the problem accurately and avoid media myths.
- **Feel where the hardness lives:** see why naive approaches and brute-force computation stall.
- **Recognize the surrounding ecosystem:** notice which fields and tools grew up around the question.

If you leave this chapter with one clean statement of a great problem, one reason it resists, and one tool or theory that grew nearby, you have already begun to think the way research mathematicians do—not as collectors of answers, but as companions of hard questions.

**Seminar note.** The deep flagship for this section is the [Riemann Hypothesis]({{ site.baseurl }}/contents/en/chapter01/01_02_Riemann_Hypothesis/). Other essays are map entries and companions for comparison when you write about open problems.

[Navier–Stokes]({{ site.baseurl }}/contents/en/chapter01/01_05_Navier_Stokes/) is the dynamics/analysis landing in this chapter.

**Optional 2022–2026 note.** [Modern Applications: Great Problems Meet Computer Science]({{ site.baseurl }}/contents/en/chapter01/01_10_modern_applications/) follows SAT solvers, neural PDE operators, AlphaGeometry, and Collatz search without rewriting the open statements.
