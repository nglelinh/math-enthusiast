Some questions refuse to die.

They look almost harmless on a blackboard—a short sentence about primes, a toy rule for odd and even numbers, a demand that a fluid equation stay smooth forever. Then decades pass. Centuries, even. Generations of mathematicians sharpen tools that were invented *because* of that sentence, and still the original claim sits there: open, or only recently closed, or solved in a way that raised new philosophical storms.

This chapter is a gallery of those questions. Not a museum of dust, but a tour of **pressure points** in modern mathematics—places where a single clear problem has forced entire fields to grow up around it.

You will meet problems that are prizes on paper (the Clay Millennium list) and problems that are prizes in spirit: the twin prime conjecture, Collatz, Kakeya. You will meet one that is already a theorem—the Four Color Theorem—and discover that “solved” can still be fascinating when the proof itself becomes part of the story.

The goal is not to solve them here. The goal is sharper and more honest:

**Understand what is being asked. Feel why it is difficult. Notice what mathematics has been invented because the question would not go away.**

If you leave this chapter with one clean statement of a great problem, one reason it resists, and one tool or theory that grew nearby, you have already begun to think the way research mathematicians do—not as collectors of answers, but as companions of hard questions.
