Classical mechanics tracks points in phase space. Quantum mechanics tracks **states as vectors** (or density operators) and **observables as operators**, with measurement statistics read from spectra and projections. The mathematical home of that language is a **Hilbert space**—a complete inner-product space—together with the **spectral theorem** for self-adjoint operators.

This lecture builds seminar literacy: why complex Hilbert spaces appear, what self-adjointness buys, how unitary evolution works, and where rigor diverges from “finite matrix QM.” It bridges [Quantum information]({{ site.baseurl }}/contents/en/chapter06/06_03_Quantum_Information/) (finite-dimensional) to continuum systems and the [mathematical physics hub]({{ site.baseurl }}/contents/en/chapter06/06_11_Mathematical_Physics/).

---

## Learning objectives

After this lecture you should be able to:

- Define a **Hilbert space** at undergrad level (inner product + completeness) and give examples $$\mathbb{C}^n$$, $$L^2(\mathbb{R})$$.
- Explain the Born-rule slogan: probabilities from $$|\langle \phi|\psi\rangle|^2$$ for unit vectors.
- State why **observables** are modeled by self-adjoint operators (real spectra, orthogonal spectral measures).
- Sketch the **spectral theorem** for a Hermitian matrix and the infinite-dimensional slogan for unbounded operators.
- Describe dynamics as a **unitary group** $$e^{-itH/\hbar}$$ generated by a Hamiltonian.
- List LO6 cautions: hidden variables rhetoric, “wavefunction collapse” vs measurement postulates, ignoring domains of unbounded operators.

**Prerequisites.** Linear algebra (eigenvalues, orthonormal bases); complex arithmetic. Measure theory not required.

## 1. From finite matrices to Hilbert space

A qubit lives in $$\mathbb{C}^2$$: pure states are unit vectors up to global phase. Two qubits live in $$\mathbb{C}^2\otimes\mathbb{C}^2\cong\mathbb{C}^4$$. Finite-dimensional QM is linear algebra with Hermitian matrices and unitary time evolution.

Continuum systems need infinite dimensions. A particle on a line has wavefunctions $$\psi\in L^2(\mathbb{R})$$ (square-integrable functions), with inner product

$$
\langle\phi|\psi\rangle = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \overline{\phi(x)}\,\psi(x)\,dx.
$$

Completeness (every Cauchy sequence of wavefunctions converges in $$L^2$$) is what makes the space a **Hilbert space** rather than a mere inner-product space. Physics calculations often ignore this; mathematical QM lives on it.

---

## 2. States, rays, and density operators

A **pure state** is a unit vector $$\psi$$, or more carefully a **ray** $$\{e^{i\theta}\psi\}$$ (global phase is unobservable). An **observable** $$A$$ that is a Hermitian matrix on $$\mathbb{C}^n$$ has real eigenvalues $$a_k$$ and orthonormal eigenvectors $$u_k$$; measuring $$A$$ in state $$\psi$$ yields $$a_k$$ with probability $$|\langle u_k|\psi\rangle|^2$$ (nondegenerate case).

**Mixed states** use density operators $$\rho\geq 0$$, $$\operatorname{tr}\rho=1$$, with expectation $$\operatorname{tr}(\rho A)$$. Finite-dimensional quantum information lives almost entirely in this language ([QI lecture]({{ site.baseurl }}/contents/en/chapter06/06_03_Quantum_Information/)).

## 3. Self-adjointness and the spectral theorem

**Why self-adjoint?** Measurement outcomes for textbook observables are real. Hermitian matrices on $$\mathbb{C}^n$$ have real eigenvalues and an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors:

$$
A = \sum_k a_k |u_k\rangle\langle u_k|.
$$

That is the spectral theorem in finite dimensions.

In infinite dimensions, many operators of physics are **unbounded** (momentum $$-i\hbar\frac{d}{dx}$$, Hamiltonian $$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\Delta+V$$). They are defined only on dense domains. **Self-adjointness** (a precise domain condition stronger than formal Hermiticity) guarantees a real spectrum and a projection-valued spectral measure $$E$$ such that

$$
A = \int_{\sigma(A)} \lambda\,dE(\lambda)
$$

in the appropriate sense. **Stone’s theorem** links strongly continuous unitary groups to self-adjoint generators—dynamics and observables speak the same spectral language.

**What rigor buys.** Essential self-adjointness questions (“is the Hamiltonian uniquely determined by the formal differential expression?”), stability of matter, scattering theory, and continuous spectrum (ionization, unbound states) are not optional footnotes for continuum systems.

---

## 4. Dynamics and the Schrödinger equation

Given a self-adjoint Hamiltonian $$H$$,

$$
i\hbar \frac{d}{dt}\psi(t) = H\psi(t) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \psi(t)= e^{-itH/\hbar}\psi(0)
$$

for $$\psi(0)$$ in a suitable domain (or by continuous extension for all vectors via the unitary group). Conservation of probability is unitarity: $$\|\psi(t)\|=\|\psi(0)\|$$.

Energy measurements relate to the spectral measure of $$H$$. Stationary states are eigenvectors when they exist; continuous spectrum encodes scattering.

---

## 4b. Noncommuting observables (pointer)

Self-adjointness makes each observable separately honest. **Pairs** of observables are another story: if $$[A,B]\neq 0$$, no state can be a simultaneous eigenstate of both, and Robertson’s inequality bounds the product of standard deviations by $$\tfrac12\bigl|\langle[A,B]\rangle\bigr|$$. Position and momentum satisfy $$[X,P]=i\hbar$$, so the Fourier fact $$\sigma_x\sigma_p\ge\hbar/2$$ is a commutator fact. The full literacy essay—Kennard, Weyl form, Stone–von Neumann slogan, energy–time caveats—is [Uncertainty principles]({{ site.baseurl }}/contents/en/chapter06/06_29_Uncertainty_Principles/).

## 5. Dirac’s delta, distributions, and honesty

Position eigenstates $$|x\rangle$$ and the Dirac delta are idealizations outside $$L^2$$. Laurent Schwartz’s **theory of distributions** and the notion of **rigged Hilbert spaces** make many Dirac manipulations precise. Seminar moral:

> Formal physics notation is a guide; the Hilbert-space theorem is the contract.

Path integrals (Feynman) offer another formulation equivalent in many settings to the Schrödinger picture, but their measure-theoretic status is subtle—see the mathematical physics hub.

## 6. What mathematics grew here

| Structure | Physics origin / use |
|-----------|----------------------|
| Hilbert space, operators | QM states and observables |
| Spectral theorem | Measurement and dynamics |
| Operator algebras (von Neumann, C*) | Infinite systems, algebraic QFT |
| Rigged Hilbert spaces | Generalized eigenvectors |
| Semigroup theory | Open systems, dissipative dynamics |

von Neumann’s axiomatization turned heuristic matrix/wave mechanics into a framework that analysis could audit.

## 7. Precision versus hype (LO6)

| Claim | Verdict | Correction |
|-------|---------|------------|
| “QM is just probability with complex numbers.” | Incomplete | Noncommuting observables and entanglement structure go beyond classical probability. |
| “The wavefunction is a little cloud of charge.” | Metaphor | $$|\psi|^2$$ is a probability density (Born); charge distributions need further physics. |
| “Infinite-dimensional QM is the same as big matrices.” | Risky | Domains, continuous spectrum, and unboundedness create genuinely new phenomena. |
| “Measurement problem is solved by the spectral theorem.” | False | Spectral theorem organizes outcomes; interpretation/foundations remain philosophical-scientific debate. |

## Exercises

1. **Inner product.** Verify that $$\langle\phi|\psi\rangle=\sum_i \overline{\phi_i}\psi_i$$ on $$\mathbb{C}^n$$ is linear in $$\psi$$ and conjugate-linear in $$\phi$$.  
2. **Born rule.** For $$\psi=(1/\sqrt{2},1/\sqrt{2})$$ and $$A=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)$$, compute outcome probabilities.  
3. **Self-adjoint slogan.** Why is a non-Hermitian matrix a bad model for a textbook observable with real outcomes?  
4. **$$L^2$$.** Why is the constant function $$1$$ not in $$L^2(\mathbb{R})$$?  
5. **Unitary evolution.** Show that if $$U(t)=e^{-itH/\hbar}$$ with $$H$$ Hermitian on $$\mathbb{C}^n$$, then $$\|U(t)\psi\|=\|\psi\|$$.  
6. **Bridge.** One paragraph connecting this lecture to qubits in the quantum information essay.  
7. **Commutator pointer.** Why is a non-Hermitian matrix the wrong *kind* of problem for uncertainty, and where do you go next to see $$[X,P]=i\hbar$$?
