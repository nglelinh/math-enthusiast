In the mid-1920s, physics faced a baffling puzzle: the very same hydrogen atom was being described by two theories that looked nothing alike. Werner Heisenberg described electrons with infinite tables of noncommuting numbers (matrix mechanics), while Erwin Schrödinger wrote down a smooth partial differential equation resembling water ripples (wave mechanics). Both predicted the exact same spectral lines in the lab! It took mathematician John von Neumann to reveal the hidden geometry: the two theories were merely **two coordinate systems on the exact same infinite-dimensional space**—a **Hilbert space**.

Where classical mechanics tracks a point moving through phase space, quantum mechanics translates everything into linear geometry: **states are vectors** (or density operators), **observables are linear operators**, and measurement probabilities are read directly from **spectra** and orthogonal projections. This lecture bridges finite-dimensional [Quantum information]({{ site.baseurl }}/contents/en/chapter06/06_03_Quantum_Information/) to continuum systems and the [mathematical physics hub]({{ site.baseurl }}/contents/en/chapter06/06_11_Mathematical_Physics/).

---

## Key ideas: What Do Functional Analysis, Hilbert Spaces, and Spectral Theory Study?

With undergraduate linear algebra (inner products, eigenvalues, orthonormal bases) and calculus in hand, the mathematical backbone of quantum mechanics rests on three clear pillars:

### 1. Functional Analysis and Hilbert Spaces
When a "vector" is no longer a 3-component arrow but a **function** $$\psi(x)$$ (infinitely many components), **functional analysis** asks which parts of Euclidean geometry survive. A **Hilbert space** is the nicest possible infinite-dimensional cousin of $$\mathbb{C}^n$$: it has an **inner product** (so lengths, angles, Pythagoras, and orthonormal expansions like Fourier series still work) and **completeness** (every Cauchy sequence converges to a point *inside* the space, leaving no missing holes). Beyond quantum mechanics, Hilbert spaces underpin PDEs (Sobolev spaces), signal processing, and kernel methods in machine learning.

### 2. Spectral Theory and Self-Adjoint Operators
Every finite Hermitian matrix diagonalizes cleanly with real eigenvalues. In infinite dimensions, however, a natural operator like position $$(X\psi)(x) = x\psi(x)$$ on $$L^2(\mathbb{R})$$ has **no eigenvectors in $$L^2$$ at all**! **Spectral theory** replaces discrete eigenvalues with the full **spectrum** $$\sigma(A)$$ (combining discrete bound states with continuous scattering states) and requires **self-adjointness**—a subtle domain condition stronger than formal symmetry—to guarantee real outcomes and orthogonal projection-valued spectral measures.

### 3. Unitary Dynamics and Distributions
Quantum amplitudes interfere via complex phases $$e^{i\theta}$$, which is why Hilbert spaces are complex. **Stone's theorem** pairs each self-adjoint Hamiltonian $$H$$ one-to-one with a length-preserving unitary rotation group $$U(t) = e^{-itH/\hbar}$$. Meanwhile, physicists' idealized objects like Dirac deltas $$|x\rangle$$ and plane waves $$e^{ipx/\hbar}$$ (which lie outside $$L^2$$) are made rigorous by Laurent Schwartz's **theory of distributions** and **rigged Hilbert spaces**.

## 1. From finite matrices to Hilbert space

A qubit lives in $$\mathbb{C}^2$$: pure states are unit vectors up to global phase. Two qubits live in $$\mathbb{C}^2\otimes\mathbb{C}^2\cong\mathbb{C}^4$$. Finite-dimensional QM is linear algebra with Hermitian matrices and unitary time evolution.

Continuum systems need infinite dimensions. A particle on a line has wavefunctions $$\psi\in L^2(\mathbb{R})$$ (square-integrable functions), with inner product

$$
\langle\phi|\psi\rangle = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \overline{\phi(x)}\,\psi(x)\,dx.
$$

Completeness (every Cauchy sequence of wavefunctions converges in $$L^2$$) is what makes the space a **Hilbert space** rather than a mere inner-product space. To feel why completeness matters, consider continuous functions on $$[0,1]$$ under the $$L^2$$ norm: you can build a sequence of smooth functions approaching a discontinuous step function, meaning the limit *falls outside* the continuous functions. Passing to $$L^2$$ seals every such hole so orthogonal projections always exist.

## 2. States, rays, and density operators

A **pure state** is a unit vector $$\psi$$, or more carefully a **ray** $$\{e^{i\theta}\psi\}$$ (global phase is unobservable). An **observable** $$A$$ that is a Hermitian matrix on $$\mathbb{C}^n$$ has real eigenvalues $$a_k$$ and orthonormal eigenvectors $$u_k$$; measuring $$A$$ in state $$\psi$$ yields $$a_k$$ with probability $$|\langle u_k|\psi\rangle|^2$$ (nondegenerate case).

**Mixed states** use density operators $$\rho\geq 0$$, $$\operatorname{tr}\rho=1$$, with expectation $$\operatorname{tr}(\rho A)$$. Finite-dimensional quantum information lives almost entirely in this language ([QI lecture]({{ site.baseurl }}/contents/en/chapter06/06_03_Quantum_Information/)).

## 3. Self-adjointness and the spectral theorem

**Why self-adjoint?** Measurement outcomes for textbook observables are real. Hermitian matrices on $$\mathbb{C}^n$$ have real eigenvalues and an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors:

$$
A = \sum_k a_k |u_k\rangle\langle u_k|.
$$

That is the spectral theorem in finite dimensions.

In infinite dimensions, many operators of physics are **unbounded** (momentum $$-i\hbar\frac{d}{dx}$$, Hamiltonian $$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\Delta+V$$). They are defined only on dense domains. **Self-adjointness** (a precise domain condition $$D(A) = D(A^*)$$, stronger than formal Hermiticity) guarantees a real spectrum and a projection-valued spectral measure $$E$$ such that

$$
A = \int_{\sigma(A)} \lambda\,dE(\lambda)
$$

in the appropriate sense. For instance, on a finite interval $$[0,1]$$, momentum $$-i\frac{d}{dx}$$ with boundary condition $$\psi(0)=\psi(1)=0$$ is symmetric yet *not* self-adjoint and generates no unitary evolution; only periodic conditions $$\psi(1)=e^{i\theta}\psi(0)$$ restore self-adjointness. **Stone’s theorem** links strongly continuous unitary groups to self-adjoint generators—so dynamics and observables speak the exact same spectral language.

**What rigor buys.** Essential self-adjointness (“is the Hamiltonian uniquely determined by the formal differential expression?”), stability of matter, scattering theory, and continuous spectrum (ionization, unbound states) are not optional footnotes for continuum systems.

## 4. Dynamics and the Schrödinger equation

Given a self-adjoint Hamiltonian $$H$$,

$$
i\hbar \frac{d}{dt}\psi(t) = H\psi(t) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \psi(t)= e^{-itH/\hbar}\psi(0)
$$

for $$\psi(0)$$ in a suitable domain (or by continuous extension for all vectors via the unitary group). Conservation of probability is unitarity: $$\|\psi(t)\|=\|\psi(0)\|$$. Geometrically, isolated quantum evolution is simply a **steady rotation in complex Hilbert space**—the length of the state never changes while its eigencomponents spin at frequencies $$e^{-iE_k t/\hbar}$$.

### Explanation: From Hamiltonian to Measurement Probabilities at Time $$t$$

Let us run the complete **spectral theorem → unitary evolution → Born rule** pipeline by hand on a single qubit in a transverse magnetic field:

$$
H = \hbar\omega\,\sigma_x = \hbar\omega\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \psi(0) = |0\rangle = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

1. **Diagonalize $$H$$ (Spectral Theorem):** Solving $$\det(H - E I) = 0$$ gives real eigenvalues $$E_+ = +\hbar\omega$$ and $$E_- = -\hbar\omega$$ with orthonormal eigenvectors:
   $$
   |+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad |-\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}.
   $$
2. **Expand the initial state in the eigenbasis:**
   $$
   \psi(0) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|+\rangle + \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|-\rangle.
   $$
3. **Multiply each eigencomponent by $$e^{-iE_\pm t/\hbar}$$ (Unitary Evolution):**
   $$
   \psi(t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}e^{-i\omega t}|+\rangle + \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}e^{+i\omega t}|-\rangle = \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix} e^{-i\omega t} + e^{i\omega t} \\ e^{-i\omega t} - e^{i\omega t} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos(\omega t) \\ -i\sin(\omega t) \end{pmatrix}.
   $$
   Check probability conservation immediately: $$\|\psi(t)\|^2 = \cos^2(\omega t) + |-i\sin(\omega t)|^2 = 1$$ for all $$t$$!
4. **Measure $$A = \sigma_z = \operatorname{diag}(1, -1)$$ at $$t = \frac{\pi}{6\omega}$$ (Born Rule):** Here $$\omega t = \frac{\pi}{6}$$, so $$\psi(t) = \bigl(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2},\ -\frac{i}{2}\bigr)^T$$.
   - Probability of outcome $$+1$$ (state $$|0\rangle$$): $$|\langle 0|\psi(t)\rangle|^2 = \bigl(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\bigr)^2 = \frac{3}{4}$$.
   - Probability of outcome $$-1$$ (state $$|1\rangle$$): $$|\langle 1|\psi(t)\rangle|^2 = \bigl|-\frac{i}{2}\bigr|^2 = \frac{1}{4}$$.
   - Expectation value: $$\langle \sigma_z \rangle = (+1)\cdot\frac{3}{4} + (-1)\cdot\frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{2} = \cos(2\omega t)$$.

In infinite dimensions, the exact same four steps govern every atom and wavepacket—only replacing finite sums $$\sum$$ by spectral integrals $$\int dE(\lambda)$$.

## 4b. Noncommuting observables (pointer)

Self-adjointness makes each observable separately honest. **Pairs** of observables are another story: if $$[A,B]\neq 0$$, no state can be a simultaneous eigenstate of both, and Robertson’s inequality bounds the product of standard deviations by $$\tfrac12\bigl|\langle[A,B]\rangle\bigr|$$. Position and momentum satisfy $$[X,P]=i\hbar$$, so the Fourier fact $$\sigma_x\sigma_p\ge\hbar/2$$ is a commutator fact. The full literacy essay—Kennard, Weyl form, Stone–von Neumann slogan, energy–time caveats—is [Uncertainty principles]({{ site.baseurl }}/contents/en/chapter06/06_29_Uncertainty_Principles/).

## 5. Dirac’s delta, distributions, and honesty

Position eigenstates $$|x\rangle$$ and the Dirac delta are idealizations outside $$L^2$$. Laurent Schwartz’s **theory of distributions** and the notion of **rigged Hilbert spaces** make many Dirac manipulations precise. Seminar moral:

> Formal physics notation is a guide; the Hilbert-space theorem is the contract.

Path integrals (Feynman) offer another formulation equivalent in many settings to the Schrödinger picture, but their measure-theoretic status is subtle—see the mathematical physics hub.

## 7. Precision versus hype

| Claim | Verdict | Correction |
|-------|---------|------------|
| “QM is just probability with complex numbers.” | Incomplete | Noncommuting observables and entanglement structure go beyond classical probability. |
| “The wavefunction is a little cloud of charge.” | Metaphor | $$\vert\psi\vert^2$$ is a probability density (Born); charge distributions need further physics. |
| “Infinite-dimensional QM is the same as big matrices.” | Risky | Domains, continuous spectrum, and unboundedness create genuinely new phenomena. |
| “Measurement problem is solved by the spectral theorem.” | False | Spectral theorem organizes outcomes; interpretation/foundations remain philosophical-scientific debate. |
