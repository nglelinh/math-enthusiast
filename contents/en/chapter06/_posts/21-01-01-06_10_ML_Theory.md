Engineering can ship a model that works on a benchmark. **Learning theory** asks a sharper question: under what assumptions does a procedure that sees finitely many samples produce a predictor with small error on **new** data drawn from the same—or a related—distribution? Classical answers use hypothesis-class complexity (VC dimension, Rademacher averages). Modern deep learning broke the comfortable story: huge models interpolate noisy labels and still generalize. The frontier is rebuilding theory for overparameterized, optimization-dependent, distribution-structured learning—without pretending the old theory was useless or that the new heuristics are theorems.

This lecture is the formal twin of the Mathematics of AI flagship: more definitions, more bound shapes, same anti-hype discipline.

---

## Learning objectives

After this lecture you should be able to:

- Define **true risk** and **empirical risk** for a loss and data distribution, and write empirical risk minimization (ERM).
- Explain the role of a **hypothesis class** $$\mathcal{H}$$ and why unrestricted $$\mathcal{H}$$ cannot generalize from finite samples in general.
- State the idea of **PAC learning** and of uniform convergence bounds controlled by complexity measures (VC, Rademacher).
- Describe **overparameterization / interpolation** and why classical capacity bounds can be vacuous for deep nets.
- Distinguish generalization **bounds**, **empirical regularities** (e.g., double descent curves), and **algorithmic inductive bias**.
- Critique “theory is dead because deep learning works” and “VC dimension explains GPT” as opposite errors.

**Prerequisites.** Probability expectations; basic algorithms. Linear algebra helps. No measure-theoretic probability required for the literacy layer.

## 1. The learning protocol

Data pairs $$(X,Y)$$ are drawn from an unknown distribution $$\mathcal{D}$$. A predictor $$h$$ incurs loss $$\ell(h(X),Y)$$. **True risk** is

$$
R(h) := \mathbb{E}_{(X,Y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\bigl[\ell(h(X),Y)\bigr].
$$

Given a sample $$S=((x_i,y_i))_{i=1}^n$$, **empirical risk** is

$$
\hat R_S(h) := \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \ell(h(x_i),y_i).
$$

**ERM** selects $$\hat h\in\arg\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\hat R_S(h)$$ (or a regularized variant). The **generalization gap** $$R(h)-\hat R_S(h)$$ is the central random quantity theory tries to control—uniformly over $$h\in\mathcal{H}$$ or for the specific $$h$$ returned by an algorithm.

---

## 2. Why restricted hypothesis classes?

If $$\mathcal{H}$$ contains all functions, one can fit arbitrary labels on the sample and fail on new points (no free lunch theorems formalize limits of distribution-free learning). Successful learning needs **inductive bias**: restriction of $$\mathcal{H}$$, regularization, architectural constraints, or algorithmic bias of optimizers.

**PAC learning (idea).** A class $$\mathcal{H}$$ is PAC-learnable if there is an algorithm that, for every distribution and every target in $$\mathcal{H}$$ (realizable case) or for approximation variants (agnostic case), returns with high probability a hypothesis whose true risk is close to optimal, using sample size depending on accuracy, confidence, and class complexity—not on the unknown distribution’s details beyond the model.

---

## 3. Complexity measures and uniform convergence

**VC dimension** of a binary class is the size of the largest set shattered by $$\mathcal{H}$$. Finite VC dimension yields distribution-free bounds of schematic form

$$
\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\bigl\lvert R(h)-\hat R_S(h)\bigr\rvert
= O\Bigl(\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{VCdim}(\mathcal{H})\log n + \log(1/\delta)}{n}}\Bigr)
$$

with high probability (constants and exact statements depend on the textbook). **Rademacher complexity** measures how well $$\mathcal{H}$$ correlates with random noise on the sample; it yields data-dependent bounds and extends gracefully to real-valued losses.

**Margin bounds**, covering numbers, fat-shattering dimensions, and PAC-Bayesian bounds are further tools. They are **theorems** under stated assumptions. When applied to enormous neural net classes, numerical bounds often exceed $$1$$—true but **vacuous** as guarantees, even if the trained net generalizes.

---

## 4. Optimization enters the theory

Classical statistical learning often assumed ERM was computed exactly. In deep learning, **which** minimizer of training loss you find depends on initialization, stochastic gradients, architecture, and early stopping. **Implicit bias** results (e.g., gradient descent on separable linear classification converging in direction to max-margin solutions) show that the algorithm selects among interpolators. Theory must become **algorithmic**: generalization of the output of SGD, not of the worst function in a huge class.

Neural tangent kernel (NTK) and mean-field limits describe regimes where training dynamics linearize or particles interact; they are rigorous in limits and only partial models of practice. Feature learning beyond kernels remains a central open mathematical story.

---

## 5. Interpolation, double descent, and modern phenomena

Deep nets often reach **zero training error** even with noisy labels (interpolation). Classical U-shaped bias–variance folklore predicted disaster; empirically, test error can decrease again as models grow—**double descent** (and related scaling curves). These are primarily **empirical regularities** with growing theoretical analyses in simplified models (random features, linear regression with noise, some tree ensembles). They do **not** abolish the need for inductive bias; they show that parameterization count alone is a bad complexity proxy when norms, margins, and data structure matter.

**Benign overfitting** literature studies conditions under which interpolators still generalize. Expect Gaussian or linear model assumptions in theorems; transfer to transformers is aspirational.

---

## 6. Beyond i.i.d.: shift, causality, robustness

Much theory assumes i.i.d. samples from $$\mathcal{D}$$ and evaluation on $$\mathcal{D}$$. Reality includes covariate shift, label shift, adversarial perturbations, strategic behavior, and causal interventions. Domain adaptation bounds, distributionally robust optimization, and causal graphical models extend the toolkit. Adversarial examples show that small $$\ell_p$$ perturbations can change predictions—geometry of decision boundaries in high dimension (link to high-d geometry) meets security threat models.

**Foundation models** add: pretraining distribution ≠ deployment; prompts as algorithms; evaluation contamination. Theory here is nascent; measurement often outruns proof.

---

## 7. Online learning and minimax rates

**Online learning** studies sequential prediction with regret bounds against the best expert or best function in a class—often distribution-free. **Statistical minimax rates** characterize the best worst-case excess risk as a function of $$n$$ and structure (smoothness, sparsity, manifold dimension). These rates are theorems of mathematical statistics; they guide what is possible, not what a particular deep net achieves on ImageNet.

## 8. How to read a “theory of deep learning” paper

Checklist:

1. **Model:** linear? kernel? two-layer net? infinite width?
2. **Data:** Gaussian? well-separated? real images?
3. **Algorithm:** exact ERM? gradient flow? one-pass SGD?
4. **Guarantee type:** high-probability bound? in expectation? asymptotic limit?
5. **Constants:** dimension dependence? vacuous numerically?
6. **Claim scope:** explains a named empirical phenomenon or only a proxy?

Frontier literacy is mostly this checklist plus humility.

### Worked bound anatomy

A typical uniform-convergence argument has three moves:

1. **Concentration for a fixed $$h$$:** Hoeffding/Bernstein bounds $$R(h)$$ vs $$\hat R_S(h)$$.  
2. **Union bound or chaining** over a finite cover of $$\mathcal{H}$$, paying $$\log|\text{cover}|$$ or metric entropy integrals.  
3. **Approximation:** every $$h$$ is close to something in the cover, controlled by Lipschitz structure of the loss.

VC theory packages (2)–(3) into a single combinatorial dimension. Rademacher complexity packages them into an empirical process. When $$|\mathcal{H}|$$ is astronomical (deep nets), step (2) explodes unless you measure a **smaller effective class**: norm balls, margin constraints, or the trajectory of SGD. That is why modern theory talks about algorithms and geometries of parameter space, not only set-theoretic hypothesis classes.

### Interpolation cartoon

Label $$n$$ points in $$\mathbb{R}^d$$ with random ±1 labels. A sufficiently wide net can achieve zero training error. Classical VC folklore says: if you can fit noise, expect poor generalization. Empirically, on structured images with natural labels, wide nets often still predict well—**because natural data are not adversarial labelings**, and because SGD+architecture select special interpolators. The theorem-shaped research program is to name the structure (margins, noise conditions, kernel limits) that makes interpolation benign. Until then, “overfit the training set” is incomplete advice, and “scale forever” is incomplete advice too.

## Common confusions

| Claim | Verdict | Correction |
|-------|---------|------------|
| “VC dimension explains deep learning.” | Incomplete | Finite VC may hold yet bounds vacuous; algorithm bias matters. |
| “Because bounds are vacuous, statistical learning theory is wrong.” | Fail | Bounds are valid but not always tight or right complexity measure. |
| “Interpolation implies overfitting.” | Classical intuition | Can be benign under structure; still not free lunch. |
| “Double descent is a theorem for all nets.” | False | Empirical pattern; theory in simplified settings. |
| “PAC learning requires the true concept in $$\mathcal{H}$$$.” | Only realizable PAC | Agnostic learning targets best-in-class risk. |
| “Test accuracy is a generalization bound.” | Category error | Empirical estimate on a finite test set is not a uniform convergence theorem. |

## Exercises

1. **Risks.** For squared loss and a fixed $$h$$, write $$R(h)$$ and $$\hat R_S(h)$$ explicitly.
2. **Shattering.** Show that positive half-lines thresholds on $$\mathbb{R}$$ have VC dimension 1; argue intervals have VC dimension 2.
3. **Vacuous bound.** If a bound says generalization gap $$\le 50$$ with high probability for losses in $$[0,1]$$, what have you learned?
4. **Implicit bias slogan.** Why might the optimizer identity matter even when training loss is zero for many parameters?
5. **Double descent literacy.** Sketch train and test error vs model size with a double-descent test curve; label interpolation threshold.
6. **Checklist.** Apply Section 8’s six questions to any arXiv abstract on “generalization of neural networks.”
7. **Stretch.** Compare a Rademacher bound statement with a PAC-Bayes bound statement: what object does each average over?

### Core statement / slogan

PAC learnability linked to finite VC dimension for binary classification (fundamental theorem of statistical learning, rough statement). Uniform convergence controls generalization for restricted classes; interpolating deep nets need other stories.

### Definitions to freeze

- **PAC.** Learn hypothesis with error $$\le\varepsilon$$ with probability $$\ge 1-\delta$$ from poly samples.
- **VC dimension.** Size of largest shattered set; measures binary classifier class complexity.
